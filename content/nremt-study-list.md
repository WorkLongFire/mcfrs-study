# NREMT EMT study list

Check anything marked "per local protocol" against your own protocols and medical direction.

## Airway Anatomy & Assessment (Airway)

- **Upper vs lower airway divider**: Upper: nose/mouth to larynx (vocal cords). Lower: below the larynx - trachea, bronchi, bronchioles, alveoli.
- **Function of the epiglottis**: Leaf-shaped flap that covers the glottis during swallowing to keep food and fluid out of the trachea.
- **Cricoid cartilage**: Firm ring at the lower larynx; the only complete cartilage ring of the airway. Site of cricoid pressure (Sellick), which is no longer routinely recommended.
- **Thyroid cartilage**: Adam's apple; shield-shaped, largest laryngeal cartilage, protects the vocal cords.
- **Carina**: Point where the trachea divides into the right and left mainstem bronchi.
- **Why is the right mainstem bronchus more prone to foreign bodies/intubation errors?**: It is shorter, wider and straighter (less angled) than the left.
- **Alveoli**: Tiny air sacs where O2 and CO2 are exchanged with pulmonary capillary blood by diffusion.
- **Diaphragm**: Main muscle of inspiration. Contracts and flattens, increasing chest volume and lowering pressure so air flows in.
- **Inhalation vs exhalation mechanics**: Inhalation is active: diaphragm and intercostals contract. Exhalation is passive: muscles relax, chest recoils.
- **Normal room air O2 and CO2 composition**: About 21% oxygen, 78% nitrogen, ~0.04% CO2. Exhaled air is ~16% oxygen (enough for rescue breaths).
- **Primary stimulus to breathe in healthy people**: Rising CO2 in the blood/CSF (hypercarbia), sensed by chemoreceptors in the medulla. Low O2 (hypoxic drive) is the backup stimulus.
- **Hypoxic drive**: In chronic CO2 retainers (some COPD), low O2 becomes the breathing stimulus. Never withhold O2 from a hypoxic patient.
- **Ventilation vs respiration**: Ventilation: movement of air in and out of the lungs. Respiration: gas exchange (O2/CO2) at alveoli and cells.
- **Tidal volume (adult approx)**: Volume of one normal breath, about 500 mL (5-7 mL/kg).
- **Minute volume**: Tidal volume x respiratory rate; air moved per minute.
- **Dead space**: Airway volume not involved in gas exchange (~150 mL in adults): trachea, bronchi, mouth, nose.
- **Normal adult respiratory rate**: 12-20 breaths/min.
- **Normal child respiratory rates by age (approx)**: Toddler 24-40; preschool 22-34; school-age 18-30; adolescent 12-20 breaths/min. Ranges vary slightly by textbook.
- **Normal infant respiratory rate**: About 30-60 breaths/min (newborn 40-60; slows toward 30-50 by 1 year).
- **Normal SpO2**: About 95-100% on room air. Below 94% suggests hypoxia; treat the patient, not the number alone.
- **Pulse oximetry limitations**: Falsely high with carbon monoxide (reads COHb as oxyHb); unreliable with poor perfusion, cold extremities, nail polish, motion.
- **Adequate breathing signs**: Rate within normal range, regular rhythm, equal chest rise, clear equal breath sounds, good skin color, normal effort.
- **Inadequate breathing signs**: Abnormal rate/rhythm, shallow or unequal rise, cyanosis, retractions, nasal flaring, accessory muscle use, tripod, altered mentation.
- **Signs of respiratory distress (early)**: Anxiety, restlessness, tachypnea, tripod, accessory muscle use, ability to speak only in short phrases.
- **Signs of respiratory failure/arrest**: Slow or irregular rate, fatigue, decreased LOC, weak cry, cyanosis, bradycardia in children. Needs assisted ventilation.
- **Retractions**: Skin pulling in at the ribs, above clavicles or sternum during inhalation; sign of increased work of breathing (esp. children).
- **Cyanosis**: Blue-gray skin/mucous membranes from low O2 in blood; late sign of hypoxia.
- **Nasal flaring and head bobbing**: Signs of respiratory distress in infants and children; seesaw breathing (abdominal) is also seen.
- **Tripod position**: Sitting upright leaning forward on hands; sign of significant respiratory distress.
- **Capnography normal ETCO2**: 35-45 mmHg. Waveform also shows bronchospasm (shark fin) and CPR quality. Monitoring use at EMT level is per local protocol.

## Breath Sounds & Breathing Patterns (Airway)

- **Wheezing**: High-pitched whistling, usually on exhalation; narrowed lower airways (asthma, COPD, anaphylaxis).
- **Stridor**: Harsh, high-pitched crowing sound on inhalation; upper airway obstruction (croup, epiglottitis, foreign body, swelling).
- **Rhonchi**: Low-pitched, rattling/snoring sounds; secretions in larger airways (bronchitis, pneumonia).
- **Rales / crackles**: Fine bubbling or crackling on inspiration; fluid in alveoli (CHF/pulmonary edema, pneumonia).
- **Gurgling**: Fluid (blood, vomit, secretions) in the upper airway. Suction immediately.
- **Snoring**: Partial obstruction by tongue or soft tissue in an unresponsive patient. Open the airway, consider an adjunct.
- **Diminished or absent breath sounds**: Collapsed lung, pneumothorax, hemothorax, severe asthma 'silent chest', airway obstruction.
- **Where to auscultate (adult)**: Both sides, comparing left to right: apices (below clavicles, midclavicular), midaxillary, and bases (posterior lower back).
- **Cheyne-Stokes respirations**: Gradual increase then decrease in rate/depth with periods of apnea; seen with brain injury, stroke, CHF, end of life.
- **Kussmaul respirations**: Deep, rapid, labored breathing; blowing off CO2 to compensate for metabolic acidosis (DKA).
- **Biot (ataxic) respirations**: Irregular pattern with unpredictable pauses; sign of increased ICP / brainstem damage.
- **Agonal respirations**: Occasional gasping, slow and ineffective breaths near death; NOT adequate breathing. Treat as arrest and start CPR.
- **Central neurogenic hyperventilation**: Deep, rapid, regular breathing from brainstem injury.
- **Apneustic breathing**: Prolonged inspiratory gasp with pause; brainstem (pons) damage.
- **Tachypnea / bradypnea / apnea**: Fast rate / slow rate / absent breathing.
- **Hyperventilation vs hypoventilation**: Rate/depth too much, blowing off CO2 / too little, retaining CO2. Hypoventilation needs ventilation assistance.
- **Fruity breath odor suggests**: Ketones: diabetic ketoacidosis (often with Kussmaul breathing).
- **Paradoxical movement**: Chest segment moves opposite to the rest of the chest; flail chest (2+ adjacent ribs broken in 2+ places).
- **Hypoxia**: Insufficient oxygen at the tissue level. Signs: restlessness, tachycardia, cyanosis, altered mental status.
- **Hypercapnia**: Too much CO2 in the blood; drowsiness, headache, flushed skin, from hypoventilation.
- **Early vs late signs of hypoxia**: Early: anxiety, restlessness, tachycardia, tachypnea. Late: cyanosis, confusion, decreased LOC, bradycardia (especially in children).
- **Subcutaneous emphysema**: Air under the skin that crackles like bubble wrap; suggests pneumothorax/airway or chest injury.
- **Jugular vein distention and tracheal deviation**: Tension pneumothorax: JVD and tracheal deviation (a late, often absent sign) with hypotension and absent breath sounds on the affected side.
- **Pulsus paradoxus**: Drop in systolic BP >10 mmHg during inhalation; seen in severe asthma, tamponade.
- **AVPU and airway risk**: Alert, Verbal, Painful, Unresponsive; level of consciousness guides airway risk. Opening airways often needed at P or U.
- **Agonal gasps or brief seizure-like activity in a collapsed patient**: May be the onset of cardiac arrest. Check pulse and breathing together for no more than 10 sec; if no pulse or unsure, start CPR.

## Airway Management & Devices (Airway)

- **Head-tilt chin-lift**: Opens the airway when no spinal injury is suspected: tilt the head back and lift the chin to move the tongue off the posterior pharynx.
- **Jaw-thrust (without head tilt)**: Airway maneuver for suspected spinal injury; push mandible forward from the angles of the jaw.
- **Modified jaw thrust when it fails**: If the jaw thrust fails to open the airway in trauma, use head-tilt chin-lift; airway takes priority over spine.
- **OPA (oropharyngeal airway) indications**: Unresponsive patient with no gag reflex. Keeps the tongue from obstructing the airway; still requires proper head position.
- **OPA contraindication**: Responsive patient or any patient with a gag reflex; may cause vomiting or laryngospasm.
- **OPA sizing**: Measure from the corner of the mouth to the earlobe or angle of the jaw (some texts: center of mouth to angle of jaw).
- **OPA insertion (adult)**: Insert upside down and rotate 180 degrees at the soft palate, or use a tongue depressor and insert right-side up. Children: tongue depressor, no 180 rotation.
- **NPA (nasopharyngeal airway) indications**: Semiconscious or unresponsive patients who have a gag reflex or clenched jaw and cannot keep their own airway (e.g., seizure, AMS).
- **NPA contraindications**: Suspected basilar skull fracture or severe facial/nasal trauma, or nasal obstruction. Stop if resistance is met. Per local protocol.
- **NPA sizing**: Measure from the tip of the nose to the earlobe; diameter about the size of the patient's little finger or nostril.
- **NPA insertion technique**: Lubricate (water-soluble), bevel toward the septum, advance along the floor of the nose, usually right nostril; if resistance, try the other side.
- **Recovery position (lateral)**: For unresponsive patients breathing adequately with a pulse and no suspected spinal injury; lets fluids drain to limit aspiration.
- **Suction unit vacuum requirement (adult)**: Unit should generate at least 300 mmHg of vacuum with the tubing clamped. Limit each adult suction attempt to about 15 sec (10 sec per some protocols).
- **Suction time limits**: Adult up to 15 sec, child up to 10 sec, infant up to 5 sec per attempt (some protocols: 10 sec for all). Oxygenate/ventilate before and after.
- **Suction pressure - child/infant**: Use lower vacuum than adults: commonly about 80-120 mmHg for children and under 100 mmHg for infants. Exact figures per local protocol.
- **Rigid (Yankauer/tonsil tip) suction**: Used for mouth and pharynx to remove large particles, blood, vomit; insert only as far as can be seen.
- **Soft (French/catheter) suction**: Flexible catheter for the nose, a stoma/tracheostomy tube, or liquid secretions when a rigid tip won't fit; measure nose tip to earlobe.
- **Vomiting patient with suspected spinal injury**: Log roll the patient as a unit onto the side while maintaining spinal alignment, then suction the airway.
- **Foreign body airway obstruction (FBAO) - mild**: Good air exchange, able to cough forcefully and speak. Encourage coughing, do not interfere, and monitor for worsening.
- **FBAO - severe adult/child**: Cannot speak, cough or breathe. 2025 AHA: cycles of 5 back blows then 5 abdominal thrusts until cleared. If unresponsive, start CPR.
- **FBAO - infant**: 2025 AHA: cycles of 5 back blows then 5 chest thrusts (heel of one hand), head lower than body. No abdominal thrusts. If unresponsive, start CPR.
- **Pregnant or obese choking patient**: Use chest thrusts instead of abdominal thrusts (late pregnancy, or when you cannot encircle the abdomen).
- **Sniffing position**: Slight neck flexion with head extension to align the airway in adults and older children. Infants: neutral position (pad under shoulders).
- **Stoma (laryngectomy) ventilation**: Ventilate through the stoma using an infant/child mask sealed around it; no head tilt needed. If air leaks from mouth/nose, seal them. Suction as needed.
- **Tracheostomy patient with distress**: Suction the tube first. If still obstructed, follow protocol (e.g., remove/clean inner cannula); ventilate via the tube or stoma with a BVM.
- **Supraglottic airway (i-gel/King) role**: Not in the national EMT scope of practice (AEMT and above). Some states/agencies allow EMTs to insert them with extra training per local protocol.
- **Cervical collar and airway**: A collar must not impair breathing or airway access; keep manual stabilization and use jaw thrust if the airway needs opening.
- **Foreign body removal in unresponsive patient**: Start CPR. Each time you open the airway to ventilate, look in the mouth and remove an object only if visible. No blind finger sweeps.

## Oxygen, BVM, CPAP & Respiratory Conditions (Airway)

- **Nonrebreather mask flow rate and FiO2**: 10-15 L/min, enough to keep the reservoir inflated; delivers up to about 90% oxygen (less in practice with a poor mask seal).
- **Nasal cannula flow rate and FiO2**: 1-6 L/min; delivers about 24-44% oxygen (approx. 4% per liter).
- **Simple face mask**: 6-10 L/min; approximately 35-60% oxygen. Never run below 6 L/min (CO2 rebreathing).
- **Venturi mask**: Delivers a fixed, precise FiO2 (about 24-50%) set by a color-coded adapter; useful when controlled oxygen is needed (e.g., COPD).
- **Partial rebreather mask**: 6-10 L/min with reservoir, no one-way valves; about 35-60% oxygen. Rarely used in the field.
- **Blow-by oxygen**: For infants/children who won't tolerate a mask: hold tubing, mask or cup near the face at 6 L/min or more (flow per local protocol).
- **Oxygen cylinder duration formula**: Minutes = (gauge psi - safe residual) x cylinder constant / flow in L/min. Constants: D 0.16, E 0.28, M 1.56, G 2.41, H/K 3.14.
- **Safe residual pressure**: 200 psi is the textbook value used in duration calculations; many services change tanks at 500 psi. Follow local policy.
- **Full pressure of an oxygen cylinder**: About 2,000 psi (2,000-2,200 psi) when full.
- **Oxygen safety rules**: Keep away from heat, flame, oil and grease; secure cylinders; open the valve slowly. Oxygen is a drug and supports combustion (it does not burn itself).
- **Oxygen administration indications**: Hypoxia (SpO2 <94%), respiratory distress, shock, CO poisoning, cardiac arrest. Chest pain/stroke: only if hypoxic or dyspneic. Titrate per protocol.
- **Oxygen contraindications**: No absolute contraindication in a hypoxic patient. Avoid routine high-flow O2 when SpO2 is normal (e.g., uncomplicated MI or stroke).
- **BVM ventilation rate - adult**: Adult with a pulse but inadequate breathing: 1 breath every 6 seconds (10/min), each over about 1 sec, just enough for chest rise.
- **BVM ventilation rate - child and infant**: With a pulse: 1 breath every 2-3 seconds (20-30/min) per AHA (2020 and 2025). Older texts/some protocols list 12-20/min.
- **BVM volume goal**: Just enough to produce visible chest rise (about 500-600 mL in adults). Avoid excessive rate/volume: gastric distention, reduced cardiac output.
- **BVM two-person technique**: One rescuer holds the mask seal with two hands and opens the airway; the other squeezes the bag. Preferred over one-person BVM.
- **E-C clamp**: Thumb and index finger form a C on the mask; remaining 3 fingers form an E along the jaw.
- **BVM O2 delivery**: With a reservoir and 15 L/min oxygen: about 90-100%. With oxygen but no reservoir: about 40-60%. Room air only: 21%.
- **Gastric distention**: Air in the stomach from too much volume, force or rate; causes vomiting and limits lung expansion. Reposition airway; give slow, gentle breaths.
- **CPAP indications**: Awake patient able to follow commands with moderate-severe respiratory distress (pulmonary edema/CHF, COPD, asthma per protocol) and adequate BP.
- **CPAP contraindications**: Apnea/agonal breathing, unresponsive or unable to protect airway, vomiting/GI bleed, hypotension (SBP <90), pneumothorax or chest trauma, facial trauma.
- **CPAP mechanism**: Continuous positive airway pressure keeps alveoli open, pushes fluid out of lungs, increases oxygenation and reduces work of breathing.
- **Asthma: definition and EMT treatment**: Reversible bronchospasm, swelling and mucus with wheezing. Oxygen if hypoxic, position of comfort, assist prescribed inhaler/albuterol per protocol.
- **COPD (chronic bronchitis/emphysema)**: Chronic airflow limitation; wheezes/rhonchi, barrel chest, pursed lips. Titrate O2 (commonly SpO2 88-92% per protocol); never withhold if hypoxic.
- **Pursed-lip breathing**: Exhaling slowly through pursed lips creates back-pressure that keeps small airways open (self-made PEEP); common in COPD/emphysema.
- **Albuterol (MDI/nebulizer) assist**: Beta-2 agonist bronchodilator. EMTs assist with the patient's prescribed inhaler or give it per protocol. Side effects: tachycardia, tremor, nervousness.
- **MDI use steps**: Check right drug/expiration, shake, exhale, seal lips (use spacer if available), press while inhaling slowly and deeply, hold breath about 10 sec.
- **Pneumonia**: Lung infection: fever, productive cough, crackles/rhonchi, pleuritic pain. O2 if hypoxic, position of comfort, monitor, transport.
- **Pulmonary embolism (PE)**: Clot blocks a pulmonary artery: sudden dyspnea, pleuritic chest pain, tachycardia. Risks: immobility, surgery, estrogen. O2, rapid transport.
- **Spontaneous pneumothorax**: Air in the pleural space without trauma (classically tall, thin young males, smokers, COPD); sudden sharp pain, dyspnea, decreased sounds one side.
- **Tension pneumothorax**: Trapped pleural air collapses the lung and shifts the mediastinum: severe distress, absent sounds, hypotension, JVD. Needs ALS decompression; rapid transport.
- **Open chest wound (sucking)**: Seal with a vented chest seal or occlusive dressing (3-sided taping per local protocol). If tension signs develop, lift/burp the dressing.
- **Pulmonary edema**: Fluid in alveoli (usually left heart failure): crackles, pink frothy sputum, orthopnea. Sit upright, O2, CPAP if indicated per protocol.
- **Croup and epiglottitis**: Croup: viral, barking cough, stridor, age ~6 mo-3 yr. Epiglottitis: high fever, drooling, tripod; keep calm, nothing in the mouth, no throat exam.
- **Anaphylaxis airway signs**: Stridor, wheezing, hoarseness, swelling of lips/tongue/throat. Give epinephrine (auto-injector/IM) per protocol first; O2 and airway support.
- **Hyperventilation syndrome**: Rapid breathing with tingling, carpopedal spasm, dizziness. Calm and coach; no paper bag. Assume a serious cause (PE, DKA, MI) until ruled out.

## Pediatric Airway Differences (Airway)

- **Pediatric tongue size**: Relatively larger tongue in a smaller mouth; more likely to block the airway.
- **Pediatric airway diameter**: Narrower and more easily blocked by swelling, mucus, or blood; small edema causes large resistance.
- **Pediatric trachea**: Softer, shorter and more flexible; hyperextension or flexion can kink it. Keep infants neutral and children in a slight sniffing position.
- **Cricoid in children**: Classically taught as the narrowest part of the airway in children under about 8-10 years (in adults it is the glottis/vocal cords).
- **Infant obligate nose breathers**: Young infants (first months of life) breathe mainly through the nose; nasal secretions can cause distress. Clear nostrils with a bulb syringe.
- **Occiput size effect**: Large occiput flexes the neck when supine; place a folded towel under the shoulders of an infant/young child to keep the airway neutral.
- **Pediatric chest wall**: Soft and pliable; children rely on the diaphragm (belly breathing). Retractions and seesaw breathing signal increased work of breathing.
- **Pediatric oxygen reserve**: Higher metabolic rate and smaller reserves, so they desaturate quickly. Respiratory failure/hypoxia is the most common cause of pediatric arrest.
- **Pediatric hypoxia sign: bradycardia**: Bradycardia in a hypoxic child signals impending arrest. Ventilate with O2; if HR stays <60 with poor perfusion, start compressions.
- **Pediatric OPA sizing and insertion**: Size corner of mouth to earlobe/angle of jaw. Insert right side up using a tongue depressor; do not rotate 180 degrees (palate injury).
- **Pediatric NPA caution**: Size carefully (nose tip to earlobe, smaller than the nostril). Small passages bleed and block easily; rarely used in infants. Per local protocol.
- **Pediatric normal heart rate - infant**: About 100-160 bpm (newborns may reach 180). Ranges vary slightly by textbook.
- **Pediatric normal heart rate - child**: Approx: toddler 90-150, preschool 80-140, school age 70-120, adolescent 60-100 bpm. Ranges vary by source.
- **Pediatric hypotension: lowest acceptable systolic BP**: Ages 1-10: below 70 + (2 x age in years) mmHg is hypotension. Over 10 years: <90. Infants 1-12 mo: <70; newborns: <60.
- **Pediatric Assessment Triangle**: Appearance, Work of breathing, Circulation to skin. Quick general impression.
- **Pediatric respiratory arrest vs cardiac arrest**: Most pediatric arrests are respiratory in origin; prioritize airway and ventilation.
- **Pediatric foreign body obstruction**: Severe, responsive: child gets 5 back blows then 5 abdominal thrusts (2025 AHA); infant gets 5 back blows then 5 chest thrusts. Unresponsive: CPR.
- **Pediatric suction durations**: Child up to 10 sec, infant up to 5 sec per attempt, with lower vacuum; avoid deep or prolonged suction (vagal bradycardia, hypoxia).
- **Pediatric BVM masks**: Mask should cover from the bridge of the nose to the chin cleft without pressing on the eyes; don't compress soft tissue under the chin.
- **CPR age definitions (infant, child, adult)**: Infant: under 1 year. Child: 1 year to puberty. Adult: puberty and older (breast development in females; chest/underarm hair in males).
- **Gastric distention in children**: Common with excessive force, volume or rate; it pushes up the diaphragm. Give gentle breaths over 1 sec, just enough for chest rise.
- **Febrile child with drooling and tripod**: Suspect epiglottitis: keep calm, position of comfort, nothing in the mouth, no throat exam, blow-by O2 if tolerated, rapid quiet transport.
- **Bronchiolitis (RSV)**: Viral infection of small airways in infants/toddlers: wheezing, nasal flaring, retractions. Suction the nose, give oxygen as needed.
- **Pediatric asthma emphasis**: Wheezing, retractions, and tiring child; silent chest is critical. Assist with prescribed inhaler per protocol.
- **Altered mental status in a child: what to check**: Hypoxia first (airway/breathing), then blood glucose; hypoglycemia is common in sick children and causes lethargy or seizures.
- **Infant airway position**: Neutral position; flexion or hyperextension can kink the soft airway. A thin pad under the shoulders offsets the large occiput.
- **SIDS vs BRUE**: SIDS: sudden unexplained death of an infant <1 yr. BRUE: brief (<1 min), now-resolved episode of color, breathing, tone or responsiveness change; transport.

## CPR, AED & Resuscitation (Cardiology)

- **Chain of survival (2025 AHA)**: One unified 6-link chain for all ages/settings: recognition and emergency activation, high-quality CPR, defibrillation, advanced resuscitation, post-arrest care, recovery and survivorship.
- **Adult compression rate**: 100-120 per minute.
- **Adult compression depth**: At least 2 inches (5 cm), no more than 2.4 inches (6 cm).
- **Child compression depth (1 yr to puberty)**: At least 1/3 the AP chest depth, about 2 inches (5 cm). One or two hands.
- **Infant compression depth**: At least 1/3 the AP chest depth, about 1.5 inches (4 cm).
- **Compression-to-ventilation ratio: adult**: 30:2 for 1 or 2 rescuers (no advanced airway).
- **Child and infant ratio, 1 rescuer**: 30:2.
- **Child and infant ratio, 2 rescuers**: 15:2.
- **Infant compression technique, 1 rescuer**: 2025 AHA: two thumbs (encircling hands) or heel of one hand on the lower half of the sternum. The 2-finger technique is no longer recommended.
- **Infant compression technique, 2 rescuers**: Two thumb-encircling hands technique on the lower half of the sternum (preferred); heel of one hand if hands cannot encircle the chest.
- **Adult compression hand position**: Heel of one hand on the lower half of the sternum (center of chest), other hand on top, arms straight, shoulders over hands.
- **Full chest recoil**: Allow complete recoil between compressions; do not lean. Leaning reduces venous return.
- **Compression fraction goal**: Minimize interruptions; keep pauses under 10 seconds; chest compression fraction at least 60% (ideally higher).
- **Rescuer switch interval**: Switch compressors about every 2 minutes (5 cycles of 30:2) or sooner if fatigued; make the switch in under 5 sec.
- **Pulse check time**: At least 5 but no more than 10 seconds. Carotid in adults; carotid or femoral in children; brachial in infants.
- **Rescue breathing rate - adult with pulse**: 1 breath every 6 seconds (about 10/min), each over 1 sec.
- **Rescue breathing - child/infant with pulse**: 1 breath every 2-3 seconds (20-30/min). If heart rate is <60 with poor perfusion despite oxygen and ventilation, start CPR.
- **Ventilation with advanced airway during CPR**: Continuous compressions without pausing for breaths. Adult: 1 breath every 6 sec (10/min). Infant/child: 1 every 2-3 sec (20-30/min).
- **Where to place patient for CPR**: Supine on a firm, flat surface; expose the chest.
- **Hands-only CPR**: For untrained lay rescuers of adults: call 911, push hard and fast in the center of the chest at 100-120/min. Children need breaths too.
- **Cardiac arrest recognition**: Unresponsive, no normal breathing (absent or only gasping), and no definite pulse felt within 10 seconds.
- **AED steps (4)**: 1) Power on 2) Attach pads 3) Analyze (everyone clear, no touching) 4) Shock if advised, then resume CPR immediately.
- **AED pad placement - adult**: Anterolateral: right upper chest below the clavicle and left side below the armpit (lateral to the nipple). Alternative: anterior-posterior.
- **AED pad placement - child/infant**: Under 8 yr (about <25 kg): pediatric pads/attenuator if available; if not, use adult pads. Pads must not touch; use front-back if needed.
- **Shockable rhythms**: Ventricular fibrillation (VF) and pulseless ventricular tachycardia (pVT).
- **Non-shockable rhythms**: Asystole and PEA (pulseless electrical activity). AED says no shock advised: resume CPR immediately; ALS for further care.
- **AED safety**: Everyone clear before analysis and shock; move free-flowing oxygen away from the chest; dry the chest; remove medication patches and wipe.
- **AED: implanted pacemaker/ICD**: Do not place a pad directly over the device (place it to the side, about 1 inch away); do not delay the shock.
- **AED: wet patient**: Remove from standing water and quickly wipe the chest dry; do not delay the shock. AED use is OK on snow or a wet surface.
- **After AED shock**: Resume CPR immediately, starting with compressions, for about 2 minutes (5 cycles) before the AED re-analyzes. No pulse check right after the shock.
- **When to stop CPR**: ROSC, care transferred to ALS/equal or higher level, rescuer exhaustion, unsafe scene, valid DNR/POLST presented, or termination order from medical direction.
- **Obvious death criteria**: Injuries incompatible with life (decapitation, transection, incineration), rigor mortis, dependent lividity, decomposition. Withhold CPR per local protocol.
- **ROSC**: Return of spontaneous circulation: a palpable pulse returns (breathing may not). Support ventilation, recheck often, transport.
- **Post-ROSC care for EMT**: Support airway/ventilation at normal rate (no hyperventilation); titrate O2 once SpO2 is reliable (2025 AHA 90-98%, per protocol); vitals, transport.
- **Special: opioid overdose cardiac arrest**: No pulse: standard CPR with ventilations and AED take priority; give naloxone per protocol without delaying CPR. Pulse but not breathing: ventilate + naloxone.
- **Drowning cardiac arrest priority**: Hypoxic arrest: ventilation matters. AHA: open the airway, give 2 rescue breaths, then CPR 30:2 with oxygen; attach AED when available.
- **Hypothermic arrest**: Pulse may be slow and weak: check longer than usual (30-60 sec per local protocol). No pulse: CPR and AED, prevent heat loss, transport; shock limits per protocol.
- **Cardiac arrest in pregnancy**: High-quality CPR with continuous manual left uterine displacement if the uterus is at/above the umbilicus; early ALS and rapid transport.

## Cardiac Conditions & Medications (Cardiology)

- **Heart chambers and flow**: Right atrium > tricuspid > right ventricle > pulmonic valve > lungs > left atrium > mitral > left ventricle > aortic valve > body.
- **Coronary arteries**: Supply the heart muscle; fill during diastole. Blockage causes ischemia/infarct.
- **Normal adult heart rate**: 60-100 bpm.
- **Tachycardia and bradycardia (adult)**: Tachycardia >100 bpm; bradycardia <60 bpm.
- **Normal adult BP**: Normal is below 120/80 mmHg; hypotension in adults is generally systolic <90 mmHg.
- **Stroke volume, cardiac output**: Stroke volume: blood per beat. CO = HR x SV. BP = CO x peripheral resistance.
- **Preload / afterload**: Preload: volume returning to the heart (filling). Afterload: resistance the ventricle pumps against.
- **Angina pectoris**: Chest pain from temporary myocardial ischemia, usually on exertion; typically lasts under 15 min and is relieved by rest or nitroglycerin.
- **Stable vs unstable angina**: Stable: predictable on exertion, relieved by rest. Unstable: at rest, increasing, lasts longer; treat as possible MI.
- **Acute myocardial infarction (AMI)**: Death of heart muscle from a blocked coronary artery. Pain often lasts >15-30 min and is not relieved by rest or nitroglycerin.
- **ACS (acute coronary syndrome)**: Umbrella term: unstable angina, NSTEMI, STEMI.
- **Classic AMI signs**: Crushing pressure/tightness, radiating to arm/jaw/back, diaphoresis, nausea, dyspnea, anxiety, pale/ashen.
- **Atypical MI presentation**: Women, elderly, diabetics: fatigue, indigestion, shortness of breath, nausea, weakness with little pain.
- **OPQRST**: Onset, Provocation, Quality, Radiation, Severity, Time - pain assessment.
- **SAMPLE history**: Signs/symptoms, Allergies, Medications, Past history, Last oral intake, Events leading up.
- **EMT care for chest pain**: Primary assessment, O2 only if hypoxic/dyspneic, position of comfort, vitals, aspirin, assist nitro per protocol, 12-lead acquisition if allowed, early transport.
- **Aspirin indication (ACS)**: Suspected ACS/cardiac chest pain. Antiplatelet: keeps platelets from clumping so the clot does not grow.
- **Aspirin dose**: 162-324 mg non-enteric-coated, chewed (2-4 x 81 mg chewable tablets), per local protocol.
- **Aspirin contraindications**: Aspirin/NSAID allergy, active GI bleeding or ulcer, bleeding disorder, aspirin-sensitive asthma; full dose already taken. Per local protocol.
- **Nitroglycerin indication**: Cardiac-type chest pain in a patient with their own prescribed nitro (and protocol/medical direction). Vasodilator: lowers preload and heart workload.
- **Nitroglycerin dose**: 0.4 mg sublingual tablet or spray; may repeat every 3-5 min if pain persists and BP allows, max 3 doses total (including doses already taken).
- **Nitroglycerin contraindications**: SBP <100 (<90 in some protocols); PDE5 inhibitor use (sildenafil/vardenafil within 24 h, tadalafil within 48 h); head injury; 3 doses already taken.
- **Nitroglycerin side effects**: Headache, hypotension, dizziness, flushing, burning under the tongue. Recheck BP within 5 min after each dose.
- **Nitroglycerin storage and use**: Tablets lose potency with light, heat, air and age (keep in original dark glass bottle); check expiration. Give with patient seated or lying.
- **Checks before assisting with nitroglycerin**: Right patient (their own prescription), right drug/dose/route, not expired, SBP above protocol minimum, no PDE5 use, medical direction as required.
- **Congestive heart failure (CHF)**: Heart cannot pump effectively. Left-sided: pulmonary edema (crackles, dyspnea, orthopnea). Right-sided: JVD, peripheral edema, ascites.
- **Orthopnea / PND**: Difficulty breathing lying flat; paroxysmal nocturnal dyspnea wakes patient at night; signs of CHF.
- **Pedal edema and JVD**: Signs of right-sided heart failure: swollen feet/ankles (dependent edema) and distended neck veins.
- **Cardiogenic shock**: Pump failure (often after MI): hypotension, cool clammy skin, weak rapid pulse, possible pulmonary edema. O2, position of comfort, rapid transport/ALS.
- **Cardiac tamponade (Beck's triad)**: Beck's triad: hypotension (narrowing pulse pressure), JVD, muffled heart sounds, from blood/fluid in the pericardial sac.
- **Hypertensive emergency**: Severely high BP (often >180/120) with organ signs: headache, vision change, chest pain, AMS. Supportive care; do not try to lower BP; transport.
- **Aortic aneurysm**: Weakened aortic wall bulges. Rupture: sudden severe abdominal/back pain, pulsating mass, shock. Do not palpate repeatedly; rapid transport.
- **Aortic dissection**: Sudden tearing/ripping chest or back pain, unequal pulses or BP between arms; time critical, rapid transport.
- **Arrhythmias an EMT should know**: Tachycardia, bradycardia, VF and pulseless VT (shockable), asystole/PEA (not shockable). EMTs do not interpret ECGs; use AED, call ALS.
- **STEMI**: ST-elevation MI: usually complete blockage of a coronary artery. Time critical: early hospital notification, transport to PCI-capable center per protocol.
- **Troponin (hospital test, not EMT skill)**: Protein released by injured heart muscle; rises hours after an MI. Measured by hospital lab to confirm MI.
- **Syncope**: Brief loss of consciousness from reduced brain perfusion with spontaneous recovery. Cardiac causes are serious; assess vitals, glucose; consider ALS.
- **Atherosclerosis**: Plaque build-up in arteries narrowing the lumen; leading cause of MI/stroke.
- **Risk factors for heart disease**: Non-modifiable: age, male sex, family history. Modifiable: smoking, hypertension, diabetes, high cholesterol, obesity, inactivity.

## Trauma Assessment, Bleeding & Shock (Trauma)

- **MOI**: Mechanism of injury: the forces and way an injury occurred (e.g., rollover, fall height, penetrating object). Guides index of suspicion and trauma center decisions.
- **Kinematics**: Study of how motion and energy transfer cause injury. Kinetic energy = 1/2 x mass x velocity squared, so speed matters more than mass.
- **Golden Period / Golden Hour**: Goal of definitive surgical care within about 60 minutes of injury. Platinum 10 minutes: limit scene time for critical trauma to 10 min or less.
- **Newton's first law in trauma**: Body in motion stays in motion until acted on by an outside force (e.g., a stopped car, then the occupant hits the dash).
- **Three collisions in a vehicle crash**: 1) Vehicle hits object. 2) Occupant hits the vehicle interior. 3) Internal organs hit the body wall/skeleton.
- **Index of suspicion**: Your anticipation of possible injuries based on MOI, even without obvious signs.
- **Penetrating trauma: low vs high energy**: Knife = low energy (damage along path). Handgun = medium. Rifle = high energy with cavitation and wide tissue damage.
- **Trauma primary assessment order**: Scene size-up, spinal motion restriction as needed, general impression, then control life-threatening hemorrhage first, then airway, breathing, circulation, disability, expose.
- **Rapid trauma assessment (head-to-toe) looks for**: DCAP-BTLS: Deformities, Contusions, Abrasions, Punctures/Penetrations, Burns, Tenderness, Lacerations, Swelling.
- **Which trauma patients get a rapid trauma assessment?**: Significant MOI or unresponsive/altered patients get a full-body rapid exam. Isolated injury with minor MOI gets a focused exam.
- **Adult blood volume**: About 5 to 6 liters (approx. 70 mL/kg). Child about 80 mL/kg; infants lose volume quickly, so small losses matter.
- **Order of bleeding control**: Direct pressure, then pressure dressing, then tourniquet (or hemostatic gauze packing) for life-threatening limb bleeding not controlled. Per local protocol.
- **Tourniquet placement rules**: Place 2-3 inches above the wound (proximal), not over a joint. Tighten until bleeding stops and distal pulse is gone. Write time applied. Do not loosen.
- **Hemostatic gauze use**: Pack firmly into wound (junctional areas such as groin/axilla/neck) and hold direct pressure at least 3 minutes (follow product directions).
- **Epistaxis care**: Sit patient leaning forward, pinch the soft part of the nose 10 minutes. Suspect head injury if after trauma; avoid pinching with skull fracture/CSF.
- **Hypoperfusion / shock**: Inadequate delivery of oxygen and nutrients to tissues because of poor perfusion. Cells shift to anaerobic metabolism.
- **Three components of perfusion**: Pump (heart), fluid (blood volume), and container (blood vessels).
- **Hypovolemic shock**: Loss of blood or fluid volume (hemorrhage, burns, dehydration, vomiting/diarrhea). Most common shock in trauma.
- **Distributive shock types**: Neurogenic, septic, anaphylactic: dilated vessels make the container too big.
- **Obstructive shock causes**: Tension pneumothorax, cardiac tamponade, pulmonary embolism: blood flow physically blocked.
- **Neurogenic shock signs**: Spinal cord injury with loss of sympathetic tone: low BP, bradycardia, warm dry skin (below injury), and priapism possible.
- **Compensated shock signs**: Anxiety/restlessness, tachycardia, pale cool clammy skin, delayed cap refill, normal BP, tachypnea.
- **Decompensated shock signs**: Falling blood pressure, altered mental status, weak/absent peripheral pulses, labored breathing, cyanosis, ashen skin.
- **Irreversible shock**: Organ damage is permanent and death follows even if BP is restored.
- **Early sign of shock in children**: Tachycardia, delayed capillary refill, and altered mental status. Hypotension is a LATE sign (they compensate well, then crash).
- **Shock care basics**: Control bleeding, high-flow oxygen if hypoxic (maintain SpO2 94% or above), keep warm, supine, rapid transport. Avoid food/drink.
- **Hemorrhage classification by vessel**: Arterial: bright red, spurting. Venous: dark red, steady flow. Capillary: slow oozing.
- **Serious blood loss amounts**: Adult about 1 liter, child about 500 mL, infant about 100-200 mL.

## Head, Spine, Chest & Abdomen (Trauma)

- **Cushing's triad**: Signs of rising intracranial pressure: HYPERtension (widening pulse pressure), BRADYcardia, irregular respirations.
- **Battle's sign**: Bruising behind the ear over the mastoid; indicates basilar skull fracture (late sign).
- **Raccoon eyes**: Periorbital ecchymosis; indicates basilar skull fracture (can appear hours later).
- **CSF leak sign**: Clear or pink fluid from ears or nose after head trauma; suggests skull fracture. Do not stop the flow; cover loosely.
- **GCS: eye opening scores**: 4 spontaneous, 3 to voice, 2 to pain, 1 none.
- **GCS: verbal scores**: 5 oriented, 4 confused, 3 inappropriate words, 2 incomprehensible sounds, 1 none.
- **GCS: motor scores**: 6 obeys commands, 5 localizes pain, 4 withdraws from pain, 3 flexion (decorticate), 2 extension (decerebrate), 1 none.
- **GCS range and severity**: Range 3-15. Severe 8 or less, moderate 9-12, mild 13-15. 15 is normal. GCS 8 or less: airway at risk; BLS airway/BVM and request ALS (advanced airway is not EMT scope).
- **Decorticate vs decerebrate posturing**: Decorticate: arms flexed toward core. Decerebrate: arms and legs extended (worse prognosis).
- **Signs of increased ICP**: Declining mental status, vomiting, headache, unequal or sluggish pupils, posturing, Cushing's triad.
- **Head injury care**: Maintain airway, oxygen to avoid hypoxia, avoid hyperventilation unless herniation signs (per local protocol), SMR, rapid transport.
- **Concussion**: Temporary altered brain function after a blow; may include confusion, amnesia, headache. No structural damage on CT.
- **When to use spinal motion restriction**: MOI with altered mental status, neuro deficit, spine pain/tenderness, distracting injury or intoxication, per local protocol/selective criteria.
- **Cervical collar sizing/purpose**: Collar limits neck movement; measure per manufacturer. Does not replace manual in-line stabilization until patient is secured.
- **Airway-opening maneuver for a patient with suspected spinal injury**: Jaw-thrust maneuver without head tilt. If it does not open the airway, use head tilt-chin lift: a patent airway takes priority.
- **Flail chest**: 2 or more adjacent ribs fractured in 2 or more places, creating a free-floating segment.
- **Paradoxical motion**: Flail segment moves in on inhalation and out on exhalation. Give oxygen; assist with BVM if breathing is inadequate. Bulky-dressing stabilization only per local protocol.
- **Open (sucking) chest wound care**: Seal with occlusive dressing taped on 3 sides (or vented chest seal); watch for tension pneumothorax and burp dressing if worsening.
- **Simple pneumothorax signs**: Pleuritic chest pain, dyspnea, diminished or absent breath sounds on the affected side.
- **Tension pneumothorax signs**: Severe dyspnea, absent breath sounds, JVD, tracheal deviation (late), hypotension. Needle decompression is beyond EMT scope; rapid transport/ALS.
- **Hemothorax**: Blood in the pleural space; dull percussion, shock signs, decreased breath sounds. Rapid transport.
- **Cardiac tamponade**: Blood in the pericardial sac compressing the heart; usually from penetrating trauma.
- **Beck's triad**: Hypotension, JVD, muffled (distant) heart tones. Signs of cardiac tamponade.
- **Traumatic asphyxia**: Severe chest compression: head/neck cyanosis, JVD, bulging eyes, petechiae. Treat ABCs, rapid transport.
- **Pulmonary contusion**: Bruised lung tissue; delayed respiratory distress, hemoptysis.
- **Evisceration care**: Do not push organs back; cover with moist sterile saline dressing then occlusive covering. Keep warm, legs flexed if no contraindication.
- **Abdominal organs: solid vs hollow**: Solid (liver, spleen, kidneys) bleed heavily. Hollow (stomach, intestines) leak contents causing peritonitis.
- **Signs of internal abdominal bleeding**: Rigid or distended abdomen, guarding, tenderness, Kehr's sign (left shoulder pain from spleen), bruising (Cullen/Grey Turner late).
- **Impaled object care**: Do not remove (except in cheek obstructing airway or interfering with CPR); stabilize with bulky dressings.
- **Pelvic fracture care**: Suspect with MOI and pelvic pain; do not rock pelvis or compress repeatedly. Bind with pelvic binder/sheet per protocol; treat for shock.

## Musculoskeletal, Burns & Triage (Trauma)

- **PMS check**: Pulse, Motor function, Sensation: check distal to injury before AND after splinting.
- **Splinting rule: joints**: Immobilize the joint above and below a long bone injury; the bone above and below a joint injury.
- **Why splint**: Reduce pain, prevent further tissue/vessel/nerve damage, limit bleeding.
- **Realign a deformed limb when**: Pulseless extremity or severely angulated; one gentle attempt, stop if resistance or pain increases (per local protocol). Do not realign joints.
- **Traction splint indication**: Isolated mid-shaft femur fracture without pelvis, hip, knee, ankle injury (pain relief, bleeding control).
- **Open vs closed fracture**: Open: bone ends or wound through skin; control bleeding, sterile dressing. Closed: skin intact.
- **Dislocation vs sprain vs strain**: Dislocation: bone out of joint. Sprain: ligament injury. Strain: muscle/tendon injury.
- **Compartment syndrome signs**: Pain out of proportion, tense swelling, pain with passive stretch, paresthesia, late: pulselessness (the 6 P's).
- **RICE for soft tissue injury**: Rest, Ice, Compression, Elevation. Wrap the cold pack in cloth; apply up to about 20 minutes at a time.
- **Amputation care**: Control bleeding (tourniquet if needed); wrap part in moist sterile gauze, place in plastic bag, keep cool on ice; do not freeze or soak in water.
- **Superficial (1st degree) burn**: Epidermis only: red, dry, painful (sunburn). No blisters.
- **Partial-thickness (2nd degree) burn**: Epidermis + dermis: blisters, moist, red, very painful. Do not break blisters.
- **Full-thickness (3rd degree) burn**: All skin layers: white, brown or charred, leathery, painless (nerve damage).
- **Rule of nines: adult**: Head 9, each arm 9, chest 9 + abdomen 9 (front trunk 18), back 18, each leg 18, groin 1.
- **Rule of nines: infant/child**: Infant: head 18, front trunk 18, back 18, each arm 9, each leg 13.5 (often rounded to 14), groin 1. Older child: head about 12, each leg about 16.5. Charts vary by text.
- **Rule of palms**: Patient's palm with fingers is about 1% of body surface area. Use for scattered/small burns.
- **Critical (severe) burn criteria: adult**: Full-thickness over 10% or partial over 30% BSA (typical text); burns of face/airway, hands, feet, genitalia; circumferential; inhalation, electrical; with trauma; under 5 or over 55.
- **Signs of inhalation injury**: Singed nasal hair, soot in mouth/nose, hoarseness, stridor, burns of face, carbonaceous sputum. Consider early airway support.
- **Thermal burn care**: Stop the burning, remove jewelry/clothing not stuck, cool briefly with room-temp water (not ice), cover dry sterile, prevent hypothermia.
- **Chemical burn care**: Brush off dry powder first, then flush with copious water at least 20 min. Protect yourself (PPE). Remove contaminated clothing.
- **Electrical burn caution**: Ensure power is off/scene safe; entrance and exit wounds; internal damage is worse than it looks; expect dysrhythmias/cardiac arrest, so have the AED ready. ECG monitoring is ALS.
- **Carbon monoxide poisoning**: Headache, dizziness, nausea, confusion; SpO2 may read falsely normal. Treat with 100% oxygen.
- **ACS field triage 2021: Red criteria, mental status and vitals**: Motor GCS under 6 (can't follow commands); RR under 10 or over 29; SpO2 under 90%; SBP under 90 or HR over SBP (age 10-64); SBP under 110 (65+); SBP under 70 + 2 x age (0-9).
- **ACS field triage 2021: Red criteria, injury patterns**: Penetrating head/neck/torso/proximal limb; skull deformity; spine injury with new deficit; flail chest; pelvic fx; 2+ proximal long-bone fx; mangled/pulseless limb; tourniquet-needed bleed.
- **ACS field triage 2021: Yellow criteria, mechanism of injury**: Ejection; intrusion over 12 in at occupant site or 18 in anywhere; death in same compartment; unrestrained child 0-9; fall over 10 ft (all ages); pedestrian/cyclist/rider thrown or run over.
- **ACS field triage 2021: Yellow criteria, EMS judgment**: Low-level fall with head impact (age 5 or under, or 65+); anticoagulant use; suspected child abuse; pregnancy over 20 weeks; burns with trauma; special needs. Follow local destination plan.
- **START triage colors**: Red immediate, yellow delayed, green minor (walking wounded), black deceased/expectant.
- **START triage cutoffs (adult)**: RR over 30 = red. Radial pulse absent or cap refill over 2 s = red. Cannot follow simple commands = red. Not breathing after opening the airway = black.
- **JumpSTART pediatric triage (ages about 1-8)**: Apneic after airway opened: pulse present = 5 rescue breaths (still apneic = black, breathing = red); no pulse = black. RR under 15 or over 45, no pulse, or P/U on AVPU = red.

## Environmental Emergencies (Trauma)

- **Heat cramps**: Painful muscle spasms after exertion in heat; skin moist. Treat: rest in cool, oral fluids/electrolytes if alert.
- **Heat exhaustion**: Heavy sweating, pale cool clammy skin, weakness, dizziness, nausea, normal or slightly high temp. Cool, fluids if alert, may need IV by ALS.
- **Heat stroke**: Core temp over 104 F (40 C), hot skin (may be dry or sweaty), altered mental status or seizures. Life threat: rapid cooling, transport.
- **Heat stroke vs exhaustion key difference**: Altered mental status and very high temperature = heat stroke. Cool aggressively (ice packs groin/axilla/neck, cold water immersion if possible).
- **Mild hypothermia**: Core 90-95 F (32-35 C): shivering, cold skin, pale, goosebumps, some confusion.
- **Moderate hypothermia**: Core 82-90 F (28-32 C): shivering stops, confusion, slow pulse and respirations, stumbling, slurred speech.
- **Severe hypothermia**: Core under 82 F (28 C): unresponsive, rigid, very slow pulse; may appear dead. Check pulse longer than usual: 30-60 s in many texts (per local protocol). Not dead until warm and dead.
- **Hypothermia handling**: Handle gently (VF risk), remove wet clothes, insulate, passive warming, active warming per protocol, avoid rubbing limbs, no alcohol.
- **Frostbite superficial vs deep**: Superficial: white waxy skin, numbness, soft. Deep: hard, cold, blotchy white/gray/blue, blisters.
- **Frostbite care**: Remove wet clothing and jewelry, loosely cover with dry dressings, do not rub or break blisters, do not rewarm if refreezing is possible.
- **Immersion foot (trench foot)**: Cold, wet, nonfreezing exposure; swollen, pale, painful. Dry and rewarm gently.
- **Drowning: priority**: Hypoxia is the problem: open airway and give rescue breaths first once safe, then CPR with ventilations if no pulse. Spinal precautions only if diving/trauma is suspected.
- **Cold-water immersion factor**: Cold water may preserve survival; continue resuscitation per protocol.
- **Drowning aftermath**: Transport even if the patient seems fine: lung problems (pulmonary edema, hypoxia) can develop hours later. 'Secondary' and 'dry' drowning are outdated terms.
- **Snake bite care (pit viper)**: Keep calm and still, limb about heart level (per local protocol), remove rings, mark swelling edge and time, rapid transport. No tourniquet, ice, cutting or suction.
- **Pit viper signs**: Fang marks, pain, swelling, ecchymosis, weakness, metallic taste, bleeding.
- **Coral snake**: Neurotoxin: minimal local signs; delayed weakness, drooping eyelids, respiratory failure. Remember 'red on yellow kills a fellow'.
- **Bee sting care**: Scrape out stinger (don't squeeze venom sac), wash, cold pack. Watch for anaphylaxis.
- **Black widow spider**: Muscle cramps/rigidity (abdominal), severe pain, sweating. Antivenom at hospital.
- **Brown recluse spider**: Painless bite then necrotic ulcer over hours/days; fever, chills.
- **Tick-borne illness**: Remove with tweezers close to skin; Lyme disease: bull's-eye rash, fever. Rocky Mountain spotted fever: rash wrists/ankles.
- **Dive emergencies: AGE**: Arterial gas embolism: rapid ascent with breath-holding; sudden stroke-like signs, chest pain within minutes. Recompression needed.
- **Decompression sickness (the bends)**: Nitrogen bubbles after rapid ascent: joint pain, rash, fatigue, onset hours later. Transport to hyperbaric chamber.
- **Dive patient care**: High-concentration oxygen, position supine (left-side/head-down is no longer routine; per local protocol), keep warm, transport toward hyperbaric care. If flown: low cabin altitude.
- **Altitude illness (AMS)**: Headache, nausea, fatigue above 8,000 ft; severe forms HAPE (lungs) and HACE (brain). Descend, oxygen.
- **Lightning strike care**: Make scene safe, triage reverse: treat those in apparent arrest first. Look for burns, cardiac arrest, spinal and eardrum injury.
- **Heat stroke: field cooling methods and when to stop**: Remove clothing; cold-water immersion is fastest if available. Otherwise wet the skin and fan, with ice packs at neck, armpits, groin. Stop near 102 F (39 C), per local protocol.
- **Mechanisms of body heat loss**: Conduction (direct contact), convection (moving air/water), radiation, evaporation (sweat/wet skin), and respiration.
- **Risk groups for temperature illness**: Infants, elderly, chronic illness, alcohol/drug use, medications, prolonged exposure.

## Neuro, Endocrine, Allergy & Toxicology (Medical)

- **BE-FAST stroke screen**: Balance, Eyes (vision loss), Face droop, Arm drift, Speech difficulty, Time to call 911.
- **Cincinnati Prehospital Stroke Scale**: Facial droop, arm drift, abnormal speech. One abnormal finding = high stroke probability.
- **Ischemic vs hemorrhagic stroke**: Ischemic: clot blocks vessel (about 87%). Hemorrhagic: vessel bursts, bleed. Both need rapid transport to stroke center.
- **Stroke priority actions**: Last known well time, glucose check (rule out hypoglycemia), oxygen if hypoxic, pre-alert stroke center.
- **TIA**: Transient ischemic attack: stroke symptoms resolving within minutes to 24 hours. Warning sign; still transport.
- **Tonic-clonic (grand mal) seizure**: Phases: aura, tonic (stiff), clonic (jerking), postictal state. Lasts few minutes.
- **Postictal state**: Period after seizure: confusion, drowsiness, headache, can last 5-30 minutes or longer.
- **Status epilepticus**: Seizure lasting 5 minutes or longer, or repeated seizures without regaining consciousness. Life threat: support airway, rapid transport.
- **Seizure care**: Protect from injury, nothing in the mouth, do not restrain, suction/airway after, oxygen, check glucose, note duration.
- **Febrile seizure**: Seizure with fever in child about 6 months to 5 years; usually brief and generalized. Transport; cool without ice.
- **Common seizure causes**: Epilepsy, hypoglycemia, head trauma, fever, drugs/alcohol withdrawal, stroke, eclampsia, hypoxia.
- **Altered mental status mnemonic**: AEIOU-TIPS: Alcohol, Epilepsy/Electrolytes, Insulin, Overdose/Oxygen, Uremia, Trauma, Infection, Psychosis, Stroke.
- **Hypoglycemia signs**: Rapid onset: anxious, diaphoretic, pale cool skin, tremors, tachycardia, hunger, confusion, seizures. Glucose under about 70 mg/dL.
- **Hyperglycemia / DKA signs**: Slow onset: warm dry skin, polyuria, polydipsia, fruity (acetone) breath, Kussmaul respirations, nausea, abdominal pain, dehydration.
- **Which glucose level is hypoglycemia**: Commonly under 70 mg/dL with symptoms (protocols vary, about 60-70). Give oral glucose only if the patient is awake and can swallow; otherwise airway support and ALS.
- **Oral glucose administration**: Only if awake, able to swallow and protect the airway. Typical dose 15 g (one tube) between cheek and gum; repeat per local protocol. Contraindicated if unresponsive.
- **Diabetic: 'cold and clammy' vs 'hot and dry'**: Cold and clammy = give sugar (hypoglycemia). Hot and dry = hyperglycemia; transport and treat per protocol.
- **Anaphylaxis definition**: Severe life-threatening allergic reaction with airway/breathing/circulatory involvement.
- **Anaphylaxis signs**: Hives, swelling of face/lips/tongue, wheezing, stridor, hypotension, vomiting, tachycardia.
- **Epinephrine auto-injector dose: adult**: 0.3 mg for patients over about 30 kg (66 lb).
- **Epinephrine auto-injector dose: child**: 0.15 mg for children about 15-30 kg (33-66 lb). A 0.1 mg device exists for infants 7.5-15 kg; under 15 kg follow medical direction/local protocol.
- **Epinephrine injection site**: Lateral mid-thigh (vastus lateralis), through clothing if needed; hold 3 seconds (follow device). Record time; may repeat per protocol.
- **Epinephrine side effects**: Tachycardia, palpitations, anxiety, tremor, pallor, headache, hypertension.
- **Bronchodilator MDI/nebulizer**: Albuterol relieves wheezing in asthma/COPD; check expiration, shake, spacer, patient's own prescription with medical direction.
- **Asthma vs COPD**: Asthma: reversible wheezing, younger, triggers. COPD: chronic, smoking, barrel chest, productive cough. Both: oxygen, bronchodilator.
- **CPAP indication**: Acute pulmonary edema/CHF or COPD with respiratory distress; contraindicated if unresponsive, vomiting, or hypotensive.
- **Nitroglycerin: EMT assistance rules**: Patient's own prescription, medical direction, SBP above local minimum (commonly 100; some use 90), no ED drugs in 24-48 h. 1 SL dose every 5 min, max 3; recheck BP each time.
- **Aspirin for chest pain**: 162-324 mg chewed (2-4 baby aspirin of 81 mg) for suspected ACS if no allergy, active GI bleeding or other contraindication; per local protocol.
- **Poison routes of exposure**: Ingestion, inhalation, absorption (skin), injection.
- **Activated charcoal**: Binds ingested toxins; use only per medical direction. Not for acids/alkalis/alcohol; patient must be alert.
- **Opioid toxidrome**: Pinpoint pupils, decreased mental status, slow respirations (respiratory depression). Treat with ventilation and naloxone.
- **Naloxone (Narcan)**: Opioid antagonist; intranasal commonly 2-4 mg (per local protocol), may repeat in 2-3 min. Support ventilations first.
- **Stimulant toxidrome**: Dilated pupils, agitation, tachycardia, hypertension, hyperthermia, diaphoresis (cocaine, amphetamines).
- **Sedative-hypnotic overdose**: Decreased mental status and respirations, slurred speech (alcohol, benzodiazepines, barbiturates).
- **Cholinergic (organophosphate) toxidrome**: SLUDGE/DUMBELS: salivation, lacrimation, urination, defecation, GI upset, emesis; pinpoint pupils. Decontaminate; ALS atropine.
- **Anticholinergic toxidrome**: 'Hot as a hare, dry as a bone, red as a beet, blind as a bat, mad as a hatter': fever, dry skin, dilated pupils, delirium.

## Abdominal, Behavioral, Cardiac & Geriatrics (Medical)

- **Appendicitis presentation**: Periumbilical pain moving to right lower quadrant (McBurney's point), nausea, fever, guarding.
- **Peritonitis signs**: Rigid board-like abdomen, rebound tenderness, patient lies still with knees flexed.
- **Abdominal aortic aneurysm (AAA)**: Tearing abdominal/back pain, pulsatile mass, hypotension, syncope; older male smoker. Rapid transport, gentle palpation.
- **Referred pain: gallbladder**: Right upper quadrant to right shoulder/scapula.
- **Referred pain: spleen**: Left upper quadrant to left shoulder (Kehr's sign).
- **Referred pain: pancreatitis**: Epigastric pain radiating to back, nausea; often after alcohol or gallstones.
- **Referred pain: kidney stone**: Flank pain radiating to groin, restless patient, hematuria.
- **GI bleed signs**: Hematemesis, coffee-ground emesis, melena (black tarry stool), hematochezia. Treat for shock.
- **Visceral vs parietal pain**: Visceral: dull, poorly localized, crampy. Parietal: sharp, constant, localized (peritoneal irritation).
- **Acute MI classic signs**: Chest pressure/pain radiating to arm/jaw, dyspnea, diaphoresis, nausea. Women/diabetics/elderly may have atypical symptoms.
- **Angina vs MI**: Angina: relieved by rest/nitro in about 5-15 minutes. MI: lasts over 15-20 minutes, not relieved by rest/nitro.
- **Heart failure signs**: Left: crackles, dyspnea, orthopnea, frothy sputum. Right: JVD, peripheral edema, ascites.
- **AED use steps**: Power on, attach pads, analyze, clear, shock if advised; resume CPR immediately for 2 minutes.
- **Adult CPR rates/depths**: Compressions 100-120/min, depth at least 2 inches (5 cm), full recoil, minimal interruptions. Ratio 30:2 (1 or 2 rescuers).
- **Behavioral emergency definition**: Behavior unacceptable to patient, family, or community; can be due to medical cause: always rule out hypoglycemia, hypoxia, head injury, drugs.
- **Hyperactive delirium with severe agitation (formerly 'excited delirium')**: Extreme agitation, hyperthermia, sweating, tachycardia, pain tolerance, paranoia. Sudden death risk: avoid prone restraint, monitor airway/breathing, request ALS and police.
- **Restraint principles**: Reasonable force, adequate help (4-5), supine or lateral, never prone/hobble, reassess airway and circulation, document, follow protocol.
- **Suicide risk questions**: Ask directly about thoughts and plan; secure scene; never leave patient alone; transport all.
- **Talking to behavioral patient**: Calm, honest, nonjudgmental, keep distance, avoid sudden moves, don't argue or lie.
- **Geriatric vital sign tip**: Older adults may have blunted responses: normal-looking BP may be abnormal for them; beta-blockers hide tachycardia.
- **Geriatric: atypical presentations**: MI/infection may present as weakness, confusion or falls rather than classic pain.
- **Geriatric falls**: Consider syncope, medications, infection, poor vision; high risk for hip fracture and head bleeds (anticoagulants).
- **Delirium vs dementia**: Delirium: acute onset, fluctuating, medical cause. Dementia: gradual, chronic memory decline.
- **Polypharmacy**: Many medications raise risks for interactions, falls, and toxicity; bring/collect med list.
- **Elder abuse signs**: Unexplained injuries, poor hygiene, malnutrition, fearful caregiver interaction. Mandatory reporting per local law.
- **Sepsis signs**: Infection plus fever/hypothermia, tachycardia, tachypnea, altered mental status, hypotension, warm or mottled skin. Early recognition and pre-alert.
- **Pulmonary embolism signs**: Sudden dyspnea, pleuritic chest pain, tachycardia, hypoxia; risk: immobilization, surgery, clotting, pregnancy.
- **Dialysis patient**: Fluid overload/hyperkalemia risk; do not take BP or IV in fistula arm.

## OB/GYN & Childbirth (OB/Peds)

- **Stages of labor**: Stage 1: dilation (onset to full cervical dilation). Stage 2: expulsion (full dilation to birth). Stage 3: placental delivery.
- **Signs of imminent delivery**: Crowning, urge to push/bear down, contractions 2 minutes or less apart, bloody show, rupture of membranes.
- **Crowning**: Baby's head visible at vaginal opening during contraction. Prepare for delivery; do not delay delivery to transport.
- **Contraction timing**: Frequency: start of one to start of next. Duration: start to end of one. Active labor: 2-3 minutes apart, 45-60 s each.
- **Questions to ask in labor**: Due date, first baby?, prior births/C-section, contractions timing, water broke, urge to push, bleeding, multiples, prenatal care.
- **Gravida vs para**: Gravida: number of pregnancies. Para: number of births (viable deliveries).
- **Delivery steps**: Support the head, check for cord around neck, suction mouth then nose only if airway is obstructed, guide head down for upper shoulder then up for lower; support the slippery newborn.
- **Cord around neck**: Gently slip over head or shoulder; if too tight, clamp in two places and cut between.
- **Cord clamping: timing and placement**: Vigorous newborn: wait at least 60 s (AHA 2025). First clamp about 6 in from baby (texts vary, 4-10 in), second 2-3 in from the first; cut between. Follow local protocol.
- **Placenta delivery**: Usually within 30 minutes; do not pull on cord. Transport placenta in bag with mother.
- **Postpartum hemorrhage care**: Massage uterus (fundus) firmly, place newborn to breast, treat shock, rapid transport.
- **Normal blood loss at delivery**: About 500 mL is normal; more than that is excessive.
- **Breech presentation**: Buttocks or feet first; high risk. Do not pull; support and rapid transport; if head stuck, make airway space with fingers (V-shape).
- **Prolapsed umbilical cord**: Cord appears in vagina before baby. Mother knee-chest or Trendelenburg, gloved hand lifts presenting part off cord, keep cord moist, rapid transport.
- **Limb presentation**: Arm or leg first; cannot be delivered in field; rapid transport, knee-chest.
- **Multiple births**: Expect more babies; call ALS; keep each baby warm; second delivery usually quick; increased bleeding risk.
- **Placenta previa**: Placenta covers cervix: painless bright red vaginal bleeding in third trimester.
- **Abruptio placentae**: Placenta separates early: painful bleeding (may be hidden), rigid tender uterus, fetal distress; trauma/hypertension risk.
- **Previa vs abruption**: Previa = painless, bright red. Abruption = painful, dark, rigid uterus.
- **Ectopic pregnancy**: Implantation outside uterus (usually tube): lower abdominal pain, vaginal bleeding, shock, missed period; life-threatening.
- **Preeclampsia signs**: After 20 weeks: hypertension, headache, visual changes, edema, upper abdominal pain.
- **Eclampsia**: Seizures in pregnancy/preeclampsia. Care: left lateral, airway, oxygen, dim lights, rapid transport, ALS (magnesium).
- **Supine hypotensive syndrome**: Uterus compresses inferior vena cava when lying flat after about 20 weeks; place mother on left side or tilt uterus left.
- **Trauma in pregnancy**: Treat the mother first; high-flow oxygen, left lateral tilt on backboard, fetal loss risk from placental abruption.
- **Spontaneous abortion (miscarriage)**: Loss before 20 weeks: cramping, bleeding, tissue. Care: pads (no packing), keep tissue, emotional support.
- **Normal changes in pregnancy**: Increased heart rate and blood volume, decreased BP in 2nd trimester, increased respiratory rate.
- **Vaginal bleeding care**: Pads, not packing; save clots/tissue; treat shock; ask about pregnancy.
- **Sexual assault care**: Preserve evidence (do not wash, change, or clean), be nonjudgmental, privacy, same-gender provider if possible, notify law enforcement per protocol.
- **Pelvic inflammatory disease (PID)**: Infection of female organs: lower abdominal pain, fever, discharge.

## Newborn & Pediatrics (OB/Peds)

- **APGAR: five parts**: Appearance (color), Pulse, Grimace (reflex irritability), Activity (muscle tone), Respirations. Each scored 0-2.
- **APGAR: Appearance scores**: 0 blue/pale all over, 1 pink body with blue extremities, 2 completely pink.
- **APGAR: Pulse scores**: 0 absent, 1 under 100/min, 2 at 100/min or more.
- **APGAR: Grimace scores**: 0 no response, 1 grimace/weak cry, 2 cough, sneeze or strong cry.
- **APGAR: Activity scores**: 0 limp/flaccid, 1 some flexion of extremities, 2 active motion.
- **APGAR: Respirations scores**: 0 absent, 1 slow/irregular/weak cry, 2 good strong cry.
- **APGAR timing and interpretation**: Scored at 1 and 5 minutes. 7-10 normal, 4-6 moderately depressed, 0-3 severely depressed. Do not delay resuscitation to score.
- **Newborn initial care**: Dry, warm (wrap, cover head), position airway, suction mouth then nose only if secretions obstruct, stimulate by rubbing back/flicking feet.
- **Newborn resuscitation: when to ventilate**: Apnea/gasping or heart rate under 100 after drying, warming and stimulating: BVM ventilations at 40-60 breaths/min with room air first. Recheck heart rate after 30 s.
- **Newborn compressions**: Heart rate under 60 despite at least 30 s of effective ventilation: 3:1 ratio (90 compressions + 30 breaths per min), two thumbs on lower third of sternum, depth 1/3 of chest.
- **Normal newborn vitals**: Heart rate about 120-160, respirations 40-60, temp maintained; acrocyanosis (blue hands/feet) is common.
- **Meconium in amniotic fluid**: Green/brown fluid; if baby not vigorous suction and resuscitate per protocol; avoid routine deep suctioning.
- **Pediatric normal HR (awake)**: Infant 100-160, toddler 90-150, preschool 80-140, school age 70-120, adolescent 60-100. Approximate: ranges vary by textbook.
- **Pediatric normal respirations**: Infant 30-60, toddler 24-40, preschool 22-34, school age 18-30, adolescent 12-16. Approximate: ranges vary by textbook.
- **Pediatric normal systolic BP**: Infant 70-95, toddler 80-100, preschool 80-100, school age 80-110, adolescent 110-130 (tables vary). Hypotension age 1-10: SBP under 70 + 2 x age in years.
- **Signs of respiratory distress in children**: Nasal flaring, retractions (intercostal/supraclavicular), grunting, head bobbing, seesaw breathing, stridor.
- **Signs of pediatric respiratory failure (late/ominous)**: Altered mental status, slowing or irregular respirations, poor tone, cyanosis. Bradycardia in a child is hypoxia until proven otherwise: ventilate with BVM.
- **Croup**: Viral, gradual onset, barky seal-like cough, stridor on inspiration, low fever, 6 months to 3 years; humidified O2, calm child.
- **Epiglottitis**: Bacterial; rapid onset, high fever, drooling, tripod position, muffled voice, no cough. Keep calm, do not examine throat, oxygen blow-by, rapid transport.
- **Croup vs epiglottitis summary**: Croup: barky cough, no drooling, gradual. Epiglottitis: drooling, toxic ill, sudden, minimal cough.
- **Foreign body airway obstruction: infant vs child (AHA 2025)**: Infant: 5 back blows then 5 chest thrusts. Child: 5 back blows alternating with 5 abdominal thrusts (2025 change; older texts say thrusts only). Unresponsive: start CPR.
- **Pediatric CPR ratios**: 1 rescuer 30:2; 2 rescuers 15:2; 100-120/min; depth 1/3 of chest (about 1.5 in infant, 2 in child). Infant: 2 thumbs-encircling or heel of 1 hand (AHA 2025: no 2-finger).
- **Pediatric dosing/airway sizing tools**: Length-based tape (Broselow) for equipment/doses. Oral airway: corner of mouth to earlobe/angle of jaw; in children insert tip-down with a tongue depressor, without rotating.
- **Pediatric airway differences**: Larger tongue, narrower airway, floppy trachea, belly breathers; use padding under shoulders to neutral/sniffing position.
- **SIDS**: Sudden unexplained death of infant under 1 year, usually during sleep, peak 2-4 months. Attempt resuscitation unless obvious death signs; support family.
- **Febrile seizure (peds)**: Typically 6 months to 5 years with rapid temp rise; usually under 5 minutes. Protect airway, remove excess clothing (no ice or cold baths), transport.
- **Pediatric dehydration signs**: Sunken fontanelle/eyes, no tears, dry mucosa, fewer diapers, lethargy, delayed cap refill.
- **Child abuse red flags**: Injury inconsistent with story, delay seeking care, multiple bruises at different stages, patterned marks, shaken baby signs. Report per law.
- **Pediatric burns/falls caution**: Use a pediatric burn chart (head is a larger % of BSA); large heavy head makes head injury likely in falls; pad under the torso to keep the spine neutral during SMR.
- **Pediatric hypoglycemia**: Common in sick infants; check glucose; altered mental status or poor feeding.
- **Pediatric poisoning**: Common household toxins; call poison control 1-800-222-1222 and get substance, amount, time.

## Medical Terminology (Foundations)

- **Prefix: hyper-**: Above, excessive (hypertension)
- **Prefix: hypo-**: Below, deficient (hypoglycemia)
- **Prefix: brady- / tachy-**: Brady- = slow; tachy- = fast (bradycardia, tachypnea)
- **Prefix: a- / an-**: Without, absence of (apnea, anemia)
- **Prefix: dys-**: Difficult, painful, abnormal (dyspnea, dysuria)
- **Prefix: poly- / oligo-**: Poly- = many, much (polyuria); oligo- = few, scanty (oliguria)
- **Prefix: hemi- / peri-**: Hemi- = half (hemiplegia); peri- = around (pericardium)
- **Prefix: endo- / epi- / sub-**: Endo- = within; epi- = upon/above; sub- = below/under (subcutaneous)
- **Prefix: inter- vs intra-**: Inter- = between; intra- = within (intravenous)
- **Suffix: -itis**: Inflammation (appendicitis)
- **Suffix: -ectomy / -otomy / -ostomy**: -ectomy = surgical removal; -otomy = cutting into; -ostomy = creating an opening
- **Suffix: -emia**: Blood condition (hypoxemia, anemia)
- **Suffix: -algia**: Pain (neuralgia)
- **Suffix: -pnea**: Breathing (apnea, dyspnea, tachypnea)
- **Suffix: -megaly / -oma**: -megaly = enlargement (cardiomegaly); -oma = tumor/mass
- **Suffix: -osis / -penia**: -osis = abnormal condition (cyanosis); -penia = deficiency (leukopenia)
- **Suffix: -rrhage / -rrhea**: -rrhage = bursting forth/bleeding (hemorrhage); -rrhea = flow/discharge (diarrhea)
- **Root: cardi/o, pulmon/o, hepat/o**: Heart, lung, liver
- **Root: nephr/o, gastr/o, cephal/o**: Kidney, stomach, head
- **Root: cyan/o, hem/o, derm/o**: Blue, blood, skin
- **Anterior vs posterior**: Anterior (ventral) = front of body; posterior (dorsal) = back
- **Superior vs inferior**: Superior = toward the head (above); inferior = toward the feet (below)
- **Medial vs lateral**: Medial = toward the midline; lateral = away from the midline, toward the side
- **Proximal vs distal**: Proximal = closer to the trunk/point of attachment; distal = farther from it
- **Ipsilateral vs contralateral**: Ipsilateral = same side of the body; contralateral = opposite side
- **Palmar vs plantar**: Palmar = palm of the hand; plantar = sole of the foot
- **Sagittal (midsagittal) plane**: Vertical plane dividing body into right and left; midsagittal splits it into equal halves
- **Frontal (coronal) plane**: Vertical plane dividing body into anterior (front) and posterior (back) parts
- **Transverse (axial) plane**: Horizontal plane dividing body into superior (upper) and inferior (lower) parts
- **Supine vs prone**: Supine = lying face up on back; prone = lying face down
- **Fowler's position**: Sitting up, about 45-60 degrees (high Fowler's more upright); semi-Fowler's about 30-45
- **Trendelenburg position**: Supine on an incline with head lower than feet; modified = flat with legs raised. Rarely used for shock now; follow local protocol
- **Recovery position**: Lateral recumbent (on side) to protect the airway of an unresponsive, breathing patient without spinal injury

## Anatomy & Physiology (Foundations)

- **Blood flow through the heart: right side**: Vena cavae > right atrium > tricuspid valve > right ventricle > pulmonic valve > pulmonary artery > lungs
- **Blood flow: left side**: Lungs > pulmonary veins > left atrium > mitral (bicuspid) valve > left ventricle > aortic valve > aorta > body
- **Arteries vs veins**: Arteries carry blood away from the heart (usually oxygenated); veins return blood to the heart. Exception: pulmonary vessels
- **Perfusion**: Delivery of oxygen and nutrients to cells and removal of waste products; inadequate perfusion = shock
- **Cardiac output formula**: Cardiac output = heart rate x stroke volume
- **Blood components**: Red cells (carry O2), white cells (immunity), platelets (clotting), plasma (liquid portion)
- **Skin layers**: Epidermis (outer), dermis (middle: nerves, vessels, glands), subcutaneous tissue (fat, deepest)
- **Upper airway path**: Nose/mouth > pharynx > larynx (epiglottis, vocal cords) > trachea
- **Lower airway path**: Trachea > mainstem bronchi > bronchioles > alveoli (site of gas exchange)
- **Mechanics of inhalation**: Diaphragm contracts and flattens, ribs lift; chest volume rises, pressure falls, air flows in (active); exhalation is passive
- **Oxygen in room air vs exhaled air**: Room air about 21% O2; exhaled air about 16% O2 (enough for rescue breathing)
- **Gas exchange**: Oxygen and CO2 diffuse across the alveolar-capillary membrane by concentration gradient
- **Aerobic vs anaerobic metabolism**: Aerobic uses oxygen and yields much energy; anaerobic (no oxygen) yields little energy and produces lactic acid
- **Spinal column regions**: 7 cervical, 12 thoracic, 5 lumbar, 5 sacral (fused), 4 coccyx (fused) = 33 vertebrae
- **Three muscle types**: Skeletal (voluntary), smooth (involuntary, in organs/vessels), cardiac (involuntary, heart only)
- **Central vs peripheral nervous system**: CNS = brain and spinal cord; PNS = nerves outside CNS (sensory and motor)
- **Sympathetic vs parasympathetic**: Sympathetic = fight or flight (raises HR, BP); parasympathetic = rest and digest (lowers HR)
- **RUQ organs**: Liver, gallbladder (also part of colon)
- **LUQ organs**: Stomach, spleen (also pancreas tail)
- **RLQ organs**: Appendix, cecum (right ovary/fallopian tube in females)
- **Retroperitoneal organs**: Kidneys, pancreas, aorta, vena cava; behind the peritoneal lining
- **Hormone that lowers blood glucose**: Insulin, made by beta cells of the pancreas; glucagon raises glucose
- **Pulse points to know**: Carotid, femoral, radial, brachial (infants), posterior tibial, dorsalis pedis
- **Cardiac conduction origin**: SA node in the right atrium is the natural pacemaker (60-100 bpm)
- **Systole vs diastole**: Systole = ventricular contraction (top BP number); diastole = ventricular relaxation/filling (bottom number)
- **Glasgow Coma Scale components**: Eye opening (1-4), verbal (1-5), motor (1-6); total 3-15, lower = worse
- **Adequate vs inadequate breathing signs**: Adequate: 12-20, regular, clear lungs, good chest rise. Inadequate: rate extremes, shallow, retractions, cyanosis, noisy
- **Layers covering the brain**: Meninges: dura mater, arachnoid, pia mater; cerebrospinal fluid cushions the brain

## Patient Assessment (Foundations)

- **Order of patient assessment**: Scene size-up, primary assessment, history taking, secondary assessment, reassessment
- **Scene size-up components**: Scene safety, PPE/BSI, mechanism of injury/nature of illness, number of patients, additional resources, spinal consideration
- **Primary assessment steps**: General impression, level of consciousness, airway, breathing, circulation (bleeding, pulse, skin), priority/transport decision
- **Life threats addressed first in primary**: Life-threatening bleeding, airway, breathing and circulation problems; treat as found
- **AVPU**: Alert, responds to Verbal stimuli, responds to Painful stimuli, Unresponsive
- **SAMPLE**: Signs/symptoms, Allergies, Medications, Pertinent past history, Last oral intake, Events leading up
- **DCAP-BTLS**: Deformities, Contusions, Abrasions, Punctures/Penetrations, Burns, Tenderness, Lacerations, Swelling
- **Pertinent negative**: A relevant sign/symptom the patient does not have (e.g., denies chest pain in dyspnea patient); document it
- **Reassessment interval: unstable patient**: Every 5 minutes (or more often)
- **Reassessment interval: stable patient**: Every 15 minutes
- **Reassessment components**: Repeat primary assessment, reassess vitals, recheck interventions, reconfirm transport priority, recheck chief complaint
- **Significant MOI examples (trauma)**: Ejection, death in same vehicle, intrusion >12 in at occupant site, fall >10 ft (all ages, ACS 2021), pedestrian/cyclist thrown or run over, motorcycle crash with significant impact
- **Rapid trauma assessment**: Quick head-to-toe exam for significant MOI/unresponsive trauma patient, looking for DCAP-BTLS
- **Retractions / accessory muscle use**: Signs of increased work of breathing; tripod positioning and nasal flaring also indicate respiratory distress
- **Crackles (rales)**: Bubbling/crackling lung sounds suggesting fluid in alveoli (pulmonary edema, pneumonia)
- **Battle sign / raccoon eyes**: Bruising behind the ear / around the eyes; signs of basilar skull fracture
- **Jugular vein distention, tracheal deviation**: Late signs of tension pneumothorax or other obstructive problems
- **Responsive medical patient approach**: Primary, history (SAMPLE, OPQRST), secondary focused on complaint, vitals, interventions, transport, reassess
- **Unresponsive medical patient approach**: Primary, rapid head-to-toe exam, vitals, SAMPLE from family/bystanders/records, then interventions and transport
- **Chief complaint**: Reason EMS was called, in the patient's own words
- **Medical alert tag value**: Gives conditions/medications/allergies when the patient cannot give history

## Vital Signs & Normals (Foundations)

- **Normal adult blood pressure**: Systolic about 90-140, diastolic about 60-90 (ranges vary by source); hypotension is usually systolic <90
- **Pulse pressure**: Systolic minus diastolic; narrows in shock, widens with rising intracranial pressure
- **Normal adult temperature**: About 98.6 F (37 C); roughly 97.8-99.1 F
- **Normal blood glucose**: About 70-120 mg/dL (ranges vary by source). Hypoglycemia treatment threshold is commonly <60-70 mg/dL with symptoms; per local protocol
- **Infant normal vitals (1-12 mo)**: HR about 100-160; RR about 25-50 (newborns 30-60); SBP about 70-100. Ranges vary by textbook; learn your course's table
- **Toddler normal vitals (1-3 yr)**: HR about 90-150; RR about 20-30 (some tables 24-40); SBP about 80-100. Ranges vary by source
- **Preschool normal vitals (3-5 yr)**: HR about 80-140; RR about 20-30 (some tables 22-34); SBP about 80-110. Ranges vary by source
- **School-age normal vitals (6-12 yr)**: HR about 70-120; RR about 15-25 (some tables 18-30); SBP about 80-120. Ranges vary by source
- **Adolescent normal vitals (13-18 yr)**: HR about 60-100; RR about 12-20; SBP about 90-130 (near adult values). Ranges vary by source
- **Minimum pediatric systolic BP**: 70 + (2 x age in years) for ages 1-10; below this = hypotension. Infants under 1 yr: <70; over 10 yr: <90. A falling BP is a late sign in children
- **Skin: cyanosis**: Blue-gray skin or mucosa from poor oxygenation
- **Skin: pallor**: Pale skin from poor perfusion/blood loss
- **Skin: jaundice**: Yellow skin/sclera; liver problems
- **Skin: flushed (red)**: Fever, heat exposure, allergic reaction, CO poisoning (cherry red; rare)
- **Skin: mottled**: Blotchy discoloration; poor perfusion/late shock
- **Normal skin condition**: Warm, dry, pink (check mucosa/nail beds in dark skin); normal capillary refill is 2 seconds or less
- **Capillary refill reliability**: Most reliable in infants and children under 6; unreliable in cold or elderly adults
- **Pupils: PERRL**: Pupils Equal, Round, Reactive to Light
- **Constricted (pinpoint) pupils**: Opioids (narcotics); also organophosphate/nerve agent poisoning and some brainstem strokes
- **Dilated pupils**: Fear, drugs (stimulants), hypoxia, cardiac arrest, certain brain injury
- **Unequal pupils**: Possible head injury/stroke (also may be normal variation or prosthesis)
- **Pulse quality terms**: Rate, rhythm (regular/irregular), quality (strong/weak/thready/bounding)
- **Respiratory quality terms**: Rate, rhythm, quality (breath sounds, chest expansion), effort
- **Pulse oximetry limitation**: Unreliable with CO poisoning, cold extremities, shock, nail polish, motion; treat patient, not the number
- **How to measure respiratory rate**: Count breaths for 30 seconds and multiply by 2 (or 60 seconds if irregular); do it without telling patient
- **Blood pressure by palpation**: Systolic only; used when auscultation is not possible
- **Tachycardia vs bradycardia (adult)**: Tachycardia >100/min; bradycardia <60/min

## Pharmacology (Foundations)

- **Six rights of medication administration**: Right patient, medication, dose, route, time, documentation (many programs add right reason)
- **Generic vs trade name**: Generic = official nonproprietary name (e.g., albuterol), not capitalized; trade = manufacturer's brand name (e.g., Proventil)
- **Indication / contraindication**: Indication = reason to give; contraindication = reason NOT to give
- **Medication forms**: Tablet, capsule, solution, suspension (shake first), gel, inhaler, powder, gas, sublingual, injectable
- **Enteral vs parenteral**: Enteral = through the GI tract (oral); parenteral = outside the GI tract (injection)
- **Medication routes: EMT vs beyond EMT scope**: EMT: oral, sublingual, inhaled, intranasal, IM (auto-injector), per protocol. Know but beyond EMT scope in most states: IV, IO, subcutaneous, rectal
- **Oxygen: indication/dose**: Hypoxia (SpO2 <94%), respiratory distress, shock; titrate to 94-99%. NRB 10-15 L/min; nasal cannula 1-6 L/min. Chest pain alone with normal SpO2 does not require O2
- **Oxygen: contraindication**: None for a hypoxic or critically ill patient; avoid unnecessary high-flow if SpO2 normal (per protocol)
- **Oral glucose: indication**: Conscious patient with altered mental status and known/suspected hypoglycemia who can swallow
- **Oral glucose: contraindication & dose**: Unresponsive or unable to swallow/protect airway; one tube (about 15 g, varies by product) buccal/oral, repeat per protocol
- **Aspirin: indication**: Chest pain suggestive of heart attack (ACS)
- **Aspirin: dose**: 162-324 mg chewed (2-4 low-dose 81 mg tablets; commonly 324 mg), non-enteric-coated, per protocol
- **Aspirin: contraindications**: Aspirin/NSAID allergy, active GI bleeding or ulcer, unable to swallow; not for children; per protocol, other bleeding disorders
- **Nitroglycerin: indication & action**: Chest pain of suspected cardiac origin in a patient with their own prescribed NTG; vasodilator that reduces heart workload
- **Nitroglycerin: dose**: 0.4 mg sublingual (tablet or spray); may repeat every 5 min to a max of 3 doses (including doses the patient took) if BP stays adequate; per medical direction
- **Nitroglycerin: contraindications**: SBP below 90-100 (cutoff per local protocol); PDE5 inhibitor use: sildenafil/vardenafil within 24 h, tadalafil within 48 h; head injury; already took max dose
- **Nitroglycerin: side effects**: Headache, hypotension, dizziness, burning under tongue
- **Epinephrine auto-injector: indication**: Anaphylaxis / severe allergic reaction (respiratory distress or shock signs)
- **Epinephrine auto-injector: dose**: Adult 0.3 mg (about 30 kg and up); pediatric 0.15 mg (about 15-30 kg); infant 0.1 mg device where available; IM into anterolateral thigh; repeat per protocol
- **Epinephrine: side effects**: Tachycardia, anxiety, pallor, tremor, hypertension, palpitations
- **Albuterol MDI: indication**: Wheezing/bronchospasm in asthma or COPD (bronchodilator)
- **Albuterol MDI: dose**: 90 mcg per puff, usually 1-2 puffs, per protocol (patient-prescribed MDI)
- **Albuterol: side effects**: Tachycardia, tremor, nervousness
- **Naloxone: indication**: Suspected opioid overdose with respiratory depression/altered mentation
- **Naloxone: dose/route**: Intranasal: 4 mg spray in one nostril, or 2 mg by atomizer (1 mg per nostril); IM auto-injector per protocol. Goal is adequate breathing; repeat in 2-3 min as needed
- **Activated charcoal (only where protocol allows)**: Adsorbs (binds) some ingested poisons. Removed from many EMT protocols. Not for altered mental status, unable to swallow, acids/alkalis, or hydrocarbons
- **Activated charcoal dose (only where protocol allows)**: About 1 g/kg (adult 25-50 g; child 12.5-25 g); shake well. Only where local protocol and medical direction/poison control still allow it
- **Pediatric medication dosing principle**: Doses are weight-based; use a length-based tape or known weight, never guess; use only protocol-approved pediatric doses and devices
- **Medications EMTs may assist a patient with**: Patient-prescribed epinephrine auto-injector, nitroglycerin, and metered-dose inhaler (albuterol); others per local protocol and medical direction
- **Side effect vs adverse reaction**: Side effect = unintended, often predictable effect; adverse reaction = harmful unintended response

## Legal & Ethical (Foundations)

- **Expressed consent**: Patient verbally or in writing agrees to care (competent adult, informed)
- **Implied consent**: Presumed consent for life-threatening emergency when patient unresponsive or unable to decide
- **Informed consent**: Patient understands risks, benefits, and alternatives before agreeing
- **Minor consent**: Parent/guardian consents; emergency treatment proceeds under implied consent if none available
- **Emancipated minor**: Minor legally treated as an adult (e.g., married, in the military, court order; in some states pregnant or a parent); can consent. Criteria vary by state
- **Involuntary consent**: Court- or law-enforcement-ordered treatment, e.g., for prisoners or dangerous patients
- **Right to refuse care**: Competent adult can refuse; ensure informed refusal, document thoroughly, get signature, witness, advise, per protocol
- **Abandonment**: Ending care without the patient's consent and without ensuring continued care by a provider of equal or higher level
- **Negligence: four elements**: Duty to act, breach of duty, harm/injury, causation (proximate cause)
- **Duty to act**: Obligation to respond when on duty or called; may arise from policy, contract, or statute
- **Standard of care**: How a reasonably prudent, similarly trained provider would act under similar circumstances
- **Scope of practice**: Care an EMT is legally allowed to provide, defined by state law and medical direction
- **Assault vs battery**: Assault = threat of unwanted contact; battery = actual unlawful touching without consent
- **False imprisonment**: Unlawfully restraining a person without consent or legal authority
- **Libel vs slander**: Libel = false written statement; slander = false spoken statement
- **Good Samaritan law**: Protects those who give care in good faith without compensation, outside duty; not for gross negligence
- **HIPAA purpose**: Protects the privacy of patient health information; PHI may be released for treatment, payment, operations, or as required by law
- **Protected health information (PHI)**: Any identifiable info about a patient's health, care, or payment
- **Mandatory reporting: common categories**: Child abuse/neglect; elder or vulnerable adult abuse; in many states gunshot/stab wounds, sexual assault, animal bites, some communicable diseases. Varies by state
- **DNR order**: Written order to withhold CPR/resuscitation; must be valid per state law and local protocol. DNR does not mean do not treat: still give comfort care
- **POLST / MOLST**: Portable medical orders for seriously ill patients detailing limits of treatment; follow per protocol
- **Advance directive / living will**: Patient's written wishes about future care if incapacitated
- **Durable power of attorney for health care**: Person authorized to make medical decisions when the patient cannot
- **Medical direction: online vs off-line**: Online = real-time contact with a physician; off-line = standing orders and protocols
- **Crime scene responsibilities**: Ensure scene safety, treat the patient, avoid disturbing evidence, document and report observations
- **Signs of obvious death**: Decapitation, rigor mortis, dependent lividity, decomposition, incineration (per protocol)
- **Documentation rule for refusals**: Record capacity assessment, risks explained, patient statements, witness, and signature
- **Organ donor consideration**: Provide care; do not delay resuscitation; follow local protocol, notify hospital of donor status
- **Patient restraint**: Use only reasonable force, per protocol and with law enforcement as needed; document why
- **Res ipsa loquitur**: Latin: 'the thing speaks for itself'; negligence apparent from the circumstances

## EMS Operations: Safety & Moving (Operations)

- **Phases of an ambulance call**: Preparation, dispatch, en route, arrival at scene, patient contact, transport, transfer of care at hospital, post-run
- **Information dispatch should provide**: Nature of call, location, patient count, name/callback number, safety concerns
- **Lights and sirens use**: Only when appropriate per local protocol and state law; drive with due regard for safety of others
- **Safe following distance for ambulance**: Commonly taught as at least 4 seconds (some sources 3); more in bad weather, at speed, or with a heavy vehicle. Follow agency policy and state law
- **Types of ambulance**: Type I (truck chassis), Type II (van), Type III (cutaway van chassis)
- **Standard precautions**: Treat all body fluids as potentially infectious; PPE includes gloves, eye protection, mask, gown
- **PPE for airborne diseases**: Fit-tested N95 (or higher) respirator for the provider when TB or other airborne illness is suspected; surgical mask on the patient if tolerated
- **Hand hygiene**: Wash after every patient contact; alcohol-based rub if soap unavailable and hands not visibly soiled
- **Sharps safety**: Never recap needles; dispose in puncture-resistant sharps container immediately
- **Exposure to infectious disease**: Report immediately, wash exposed area, document, follow agency exposure control plan
- **Cleaning vs disinfection vs sterilization**: Cleaning removes dirt; disinfection kills most organisms; sterilization kills all microorganisms
- **Body mechanics: power lift**: Feet shoulder-width apart, back straight, lift with legs, weight close to body, no twisting
- **Power grip**: Palms up, hands at least 10 inches apart, fully contact the object with all fingers
- **Emergency move: when used**: Immediate danger (fire, hazmat, explosives), cannot protect patient, or cannot access others needing care
- **Emergency move examples**: Blanket drag, clothing drag, shoulder drag, firefighter's drag
- **Urgent move: when used**: Immediate life threat needing rapid transport: altered mental status, inadequate ABCs, shock
- **Non-urgent move**: No immediate life threat; take time to stabilize and use proper equipment
- **Rapid extrication technique**: Urgent move of a seated patient from a vehicle onto a long board with manual spinal stabilization; for unstable patients, unsafe scenes, or to reach a critical patient
- **Stair chair**: Used for carrying a seated patient down stairs; not for spinal injuries
- **Scoop stretcher**: Splits and rejoins around patient; minimal movement; helpful for pelvic/spinal precautions
- **Long backboard**: Rigid device for moving spinal-risk patients; not a long-term transport surface
- **Vehicle extrication steps**: Size-up, hazard control and vehicle stabilization, gain access, emergency care, disentanglement (remove vehicle from around patient), removal and transfer
- **Extrication hazards**: Undeployed airbags, leaking fuel, battery/electrical, traffic, broken glass and sharp metal, hybrid/EV high voltage, unstable vehicle
- **Air medical landing zone size**: Commonly 100 x 100 ft (some services accept 60 x 60 ft by day); flat, firm, clear of wires, obstacles and loose debris. Follow the flight service/local protocol
- **Approaching a helicopter**: Only when signaled by the crew; from the front in the pilot's view, crouched; never near the tail rotor; on a slope approach from the downhill side
- **Scene safety for violence**: Stage away until police secure the scene; consider hidden weapons
- **Reasons for PCR documentation**: Continuity of care, legal record, billing/administrative, education, research, quality improvement
- **PCR correction rule**: Draw one line through the error, initial it, and add correct info; never obliterate
- **Radio communication rules**: Clear, brief, plain language, hold mic 2-3 inches away, push key and wait 1 second, no profanity, repeat orders
- **Verbal hospital report contents**: Age/sex, chief complaint, history, vitals, assessment findings, treatments given, ETA
- **Critical incident stress management**: Peer support and debriefing after traumatic calls; recognize signs of stress, burnout, PTSD

## Operations: MCI, ICS & Hazmat (Operations)

- **Multiple-casualty incident (MCI)**: Incident whose number or severity of patients strains or exceeds available resources. Some textbooks say 3 or more patients; the threshold is set by the local plan
- **NIMS / ICS purpose**: Standardized, scalable command structure and common terminology for managing incidents
- **Incident Commander**: Overall responsibility for incident; sets objectives, priorities, and strategy
- **Command staff positions**: Public Information Officer, Safety Officer, Liaison Officer
- **General staff sections**: Operations, Planning, Logistics, Finance/Administration (NIMS also allows an optional Intelligence/Investigations function)
- **Unified command**: Multiple agencies share command with agreed-upon objectives
- **Span of control**: Number of people one supervisor can manage: NIMS optimum is 1 supervisor to 5 subordinates; textbooks commonly give a range of 3-7
- **Staging area**: Location where resources wait until assigned
- **MCI functional areas (EMS branch)**: Triage, treatment, transport, staging; also extrication/rescue, morgue, rehab and supply as needed (ICS calls these groups, divisions or units)
- **Triage purpose**: Sort patients by urgency to do the greatest good for the most; reassess continually
- **START: first step**: Direct walking wounded to a safe area; they are tagged green (minor)
- **START: respirations**: Not breathing after airway opened = black; starts breathing after airway opened = red; rate over 30/min = red; under 30 = go on to check perfusion
- **START: perfusion**: No radial pulse or capillary refill >2 seconds = red; control severe bleeding; if pulse present/refill normal, check mental status
- **START: mental status**: Cannot follow simple commands = red; can follow commands = yellow
- **START tag colors**: Red = immediate; yellow = delayed; green = minor; black = deceased/expectant
- **JumpSTART age range**: Pediatric MCI triage for about ages 1-8 (if the patient looks like a child use JumpSTART; like a young adult use START). Infants are assessed with the non-ambulatory steps
- **JumpSTART: ambulatory**: Children able to walk are tagged green (minor) and sent for secondary triage; infants and children who cannot normally walk are assessed with the full algorithm
- **JumpSTART: apnea**: Open airway. Breathing starts = red. Still apneic: check pulse. No pulse = black. Pulse present: give 5 rescue breaths; breathing = red, still apneic = black
- **JumpSTART: respirations**: Rate under 15 or over 45/min, or irregular = red; rate 15-45 = go on to assess perfusion
- **JumpSTART: perfusion & mental status**: No palpable pulse = red. AVPU: P with inappropriate response (posturing) or U = red; A, V, or P with appropriate response (localizes pain) = yellow
- **JumpSTART: black criteria**: Apneic with no palpable pulse after airway opening, OR pulse present but still apneic after 5 rescue breaths = black (deceased/expectant)
- **Hot zone**: Contaminated area; only trained hazmat personnel in proper PPE enter; also called exclusion (red) zone
- **Warm zone**: Contamination reduction zone between hot and cold; decontamination corridor is here; entry needs proper PPE and training
- **Cold zone**: Safe support zone (uphill/upwind): command post, staging, and EMS triage/treatment of decontaminated patients
- **ERG: yellow pages**: Index of materials in order of 4-digit UN/NA ID number; gives the guide number. Highlighted entries: go to green pages
- **ERG: blue pages**: Index of materials alphabetically by name; gives ID number and guide number. Highlighted entries: go to green pages
- **ERG: orange pages**: The numbered guides: potential hazards, public safety (isolation, PPE, evacuation), and emergency response (fire, spill, first aid)
- **ERG: green pages**: Tables of initial isolation and protective action distances for toxic-inhalation-hazard and water-reactive materials (highlighted in yellow/blue pages)
- **Hazmat placard color: red / orange**: Red = flammable; orange = explosive
- **Hazmat placard color: green / yellow / blue**: Green = nonflammable gas; yellow = oxidizer; blue = dangerous when wet. (Yellow over white = radioactive)
- **Hazmat placard color: white**: White = poison/toxic or infectious substance; white top with black bottom = corrosive; red and white stripes = flammable solid
- **NFPA 704 diamond colors**: Blue = health, red = flammability, yellow = instability (reactivity), white = special hazards (e.g., W = no water, OX); each rated 0-4, 4 = worst
- **Approach to hazmat scene**: Approach from uphill and upwind (upstream); stay at a safe distance; identify with binoculars; isolate and deny entry; do not enter without training/PPE
- **Resources for hazmat identification**: Placards, shipping papers, safety data sheets, ERG, CHEMTREC, poison control

## Radio Systems & What to Say (Communication)

- **Simplex radio**: Transmit OR receive, one at a time, on one frequency. Push to talk, release to listen. You can't be interrupted while keyed up.
- **Duplex radio**: Transmit and receive at the same time using two frequencies, like a phone call. Either side can interrupt.
- **Multiplex radio**: Sends two or more signals at once on the same channel, such as voice plus ECG/telemetry data.
- **Trunking system**: A computer assigns the next open frequency from a shared pool to each transmission, so many agencies share few channels.
- **Repeater**: Receives a weak signal (portable or mobile radio) and rebroadcasts it at higher power to extend range.
- **Base station**: Fixed radio at dispatch or a hospital, high power with a tall antenna. Longest range of the three radio types.
- **Mobile radio**: Radio mounted in the ambulance. More power and range than a portable, less than a base station.
- **Portable radio**: Handheld radio you carry on scene. Lowest power and range; may need a repeater to reach dispatch or the hospital.
- **Telemetry**: Sending data such as an ECG tracing electronically to the hospital or medical control for interpretation.
- **MDT / MDC**: Mobile data terminal or computer in the rig: shows dispatch info and status buttons, cuts down voice traffic.
- **Who regulates EMS radio frequencies?**: The FCC (Federal Communications Commission). It licenses frequencies and bans profanity and false info on the air.
- **Online medical control**: Real-time direction from a physician by radio or phone, such as an order for a medication not covered by standing orders.
- **Offline (indirect) medical control**: Protocols, standing orders, training and QA set by the medical director. You act without calling first.
- **Before you speak on the radio**: Listen first to make sure the channel is clear. Press the button, wait about 1 second, then speak.
- **How to hold and use the mic**: About 2 to 3 inches from your mouth, speak across it at a normal volume, slowly and clearly.
- **How long should one transmission be?**: Short. Keep each one under about 30 seconds; break long reports up and let the other side acknowledge.
- **Plain English vs 10-codes**: Use plain English. NIMS recommends plain language, and codes vary between agencies. Avoid slang and jargon.
- **Yes / no on the radio**: Say "affirmative" and "negative" (easier to hear than yes and no). Say "copy" or "received" to acknowledge.
- **Patient privacy on the radio**: Never say the patient's name or other identifiers. Scanners can hear you (HIPAA). Use age, sex and condition.
- **What not to say on the radio**: No profanity, no diagnoses you can't confirm, no judgments about the patient, no "please" or "thank you" chatter.
- **Echo method**: Repeat a physician's order back word for word (drug, dose, route) before giving it. Confirms it was heard right.
- **Order that seems wrong or unclear**: Ask the physician to repeat or clarify it. Never carry out an order you think is inappropriate; document it.
- **Status updates to dispatch**: Call: en route, on scene, patient contact if required, leaving scene/transporting, at hospital, back in service.
- **Calling dispatch: format**: Call them, then identify yourself: "Dispatch, Medic 12." Wait for "Medic 12, go ahead," then give your message.
- **Order of the hospital call-in report**: 1 Unit ID and level  2 ETA  3 Age and sex  4 Chief complaint  5 HPI  6 Past history  7 Mental status  8 Vitals  9 Exam  10 Care given  11 Response
- **Report step 1-2: Unit ID, level, ETA**: "Mercy General, Medic 12, BLS unit, priority 2, ETA 10 minutes."
- **Report step 3-4: Age, sex, chief complaint**: "We are transporting a 64-year-old male complaining of chest pain."
- **Report step 5: History of present illness**: "Onset 30 minutes ago at rest, pressure radiating to the left arm, rated 8 out of 10, with nausea."
- **Report step 6: Pertinent past history**: "History of hypertension and high cholesterol. Takes lisinopril. No known allergies."
- **Report step 7-8: Mental status and vitals**: "Alert and oriented times 4. BP 158 over 92, pulse 104 regular, respirations 20, SpO2 95 percent."
- **Report step 9: Pertinent exam findings**: "Skin pale, cool, diaphoretic. Lungs clear bilaterally. No JVD or edema."
- **Report step 10-11: Care given and response**: "Gave 324 mg aspirin, assisted with one nitro. Pain now 6 out of 10. Any further orders?"
- **Ending a radio report**: Wait for the hospital to acknowledge. Echo any orders, then sign off: "Medic 12, copy, clear."
- **Verbal report at the hospital (transfer of care)**: Face to face to the nurse or doctor who takes over: name, age, chief complaint, history, findings, care, response and changes en route.
- **Why give the call-in report?**: It lets the ED prepare the right room, staff and team, such as a stroke or STEMI alert, before you arrive.

## Hospital Types & Destinations (Destinations)

- **Level I trauma center**: Highest level: 24/7 in-house surgeons and all specialists, plus research, teaching and prevention. Handles any injury.
- **Level II trauma center**: 24/7 surgeons and comprehensive trauma care, but without the research and teaching requirements of Level I.
- **Level III trauma center**: Prompt assessment, resuscitation and emergency surgery; transfers patients who need more to Level I or II.
- **Level IV trauma center**: Usually rural: advanced trauma life support and stabilization before transfer to a higher-level center.
- **Level V trauma center (some states)**: Basic ED stabilization and transfer in remote areas. Not every state uses Level V.
- **Pediatric trauma center**: Verified to care for injured children, with pediatric surgeons and equipment. Many protocols send seriously injured kids here.
- **Burn center**: Specialized burn care units. Take critical burns, inhalation injury and burns to the face, hands, feet, groin or joints, per protocol.
- **Comprehensive stroke center**: Highest stroke level: thrombectomy, neurosurgery and neuro ICU 24/7. For large-vessel-occlusion strokes, per protocol.
- **Thrombectomy-capable stroke center**: Can remove a clot from a large brain artery (endovascular), but has fewer specialty resources than a comprehensive center.
- **Primary stroke center**: Can diagnose stroke quickly and give clot-busting drugs (thrombolytics); transfers patients needing thrombectomy.
- **Acute stroke-ready hospital**: Smaller hospital that can assess, give thrombolytics and rapidly transfer. Common in rural areas.
- **STEMI / PCI center**: Has a 24/7 cardiac cath lab to open blocked coronary arteries. Many systems send STEMI patients straight here.
- **Perinatal / obstetric center**: Has labor and delivery plus a NICU (levels I to IV). Take high-risk pregnancies or delivering patients here, per protocol.
- **Hyperbaric center**: Has a hyperbaric oxygen chamber: for diving injuries (decompression sickness, air embolism) and severe CO poisoning.
- **Critical access hospital**: Small rural hospital (25 beds or fewer) that stabilizes and transfers. May lack specialty services.
- **Psychiatric / behavioral health facility**: For psychiatric emergencies once the patient is medically cleared. Medical problems usually go to an ED first.
- **Free-standing emergency department**: ED not attached to a hospital. Limited specialty care and no admission, so check your protocol before transporting there.
- **Poison Control Center**: Not a hospital: a hotline (US 1-800-222-1222) giving toxicology advice for overdoses and poisonings.
- **Choosing a destination**: Closest appropriate facility for the patient's condition, using protocol, patient choice, and diversion status.
- **Hospital diversion**: A hospital temporarily asks EMS to go elsewhere because its ED or a service is full. Unstable patients may still go there, per protocol.
